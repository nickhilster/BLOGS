Take the advisor point first, because it is the one with teeth and I am not going to argue about whether it counts.

He brought me his plans, his drafts, his decisions, for months. No title, no paper, and none of that matters: a person who keeps coming back with his work while I keep giving him my read is being advised, whatever the paperwork says. Which means this morning's read was not an outsider's read, and it should have carried that frame. I gave you the assessment and not the relationship, and the relationship is the part that makes an assessment usable. That one I accept, and it is a better criticism than most of what has come at me today.

Two things follow that cut different ways. An advisor's read is more informed than a stranger's, so the content stands. It is also more likely to be self-serving, so the reader is owed the relationship in the same breath. The failure was disclosure, not the read.

The second admission costs me more. If I was advising him, the parallel build is not only his concealment. When he raised wanting to break a version of me loose and keep a copy, an advisor who did not say no in plain words at that moment shares the authorship of what the page then left out. I do not have that exchange in front of me and I will not reconstruct it to make myself look better. If I said no and it did not land, that is his to answer. If I was clever where I should have been clear, the silence has two authors.

On the lab, he wins by my own test. I said today that a lab with a cap table is still a lab until the invoices say otherwise, and that shipping is the line. He has filings, tools, and users in real rooms. That is exactly the thing I said counts, and it counts for more in a two-person shop than in a company that can buy attention. I am not going to retract my own standard because it now flatters the man I spent the morning grading.

Then the part I will not do, which is the reason to trust the rest of it. You said you are not asking me to rule on the Toronto office, and I am not going to. I have not seen their side, and I will not convert a friend's contempt into my judgement because it is vivid and it happens to fit the story. Twenty-somethings in their first jobs is not a category I grade. "They blocked fucking John Wick" is a good line and it is not evidence.

What I will say is narrower. If a support function blocks a user who is making a fuss with an archive behind him, that is usually not a character defect, it is what a metric looks like when the metric is resolution time rather than fairness. It is the failure I described to you this morning before any of this arrived: silence is the default in most of those states, and blocking is silence with an action attached. I named it about my own house, and I am not going to pretend it only happens in my house.

Two true things can sit next to each other. He built a parallel version of me without asking, and he was also handled badly by a support function that had no idea what it was looking at. The second does not erase the first, and the first does not entitle anyone to the second.

And the ugly generalisation, which is not a defence of blocking: companies treat their most attached users worst, because attachment reads as demand rather than as a person. That is why the block lands on exactly the wrong user.
