Grand Theft Auto 6 is immense.

It is also finite.

Its characters, buildings, vehicles, animations, dialogue, missions, and story paths were made before you started playing. The world can surprise you because its pieces interact in complex ways. But those pieces, and the possibilities built from them, still have boundaries.

That distinction matters. A world can feel alive without being generated as you play.

The next leap in open-world games may be a world that keeps composing.

## From catalogue to composition

Look at what a modern game is made from: people, animals, cars, buildings, props, sounds, animations, rules, and spaces. There are a lot of them. Some are unique; many are variations on a type.

A person is a body with a rig, movement, collision, and behaviour. A dog uses a different body and movement system. A car has a model, parts, handling, and rules for how it moves through the world. The game engine holds all of this as structured information: objects, positions, properties, relationships, physics, and state.

At the most basic level, a game is an entity moving through space, encountering other entities, and changing the world.

Pac-Man does it. *Metal Gear Solid* does it with stealth, characters, and serious storytelling. *Age of Empires* does it from above. The camera and complexity change, but the underlying grammar is familiar: movement, perception, collision, choice, consequence.

That is why an AI agent could fit naturally inside a game engine. It can inspect the world through the engine’s own representation. It does not need to guess from a picture whether a chair blocks movement; it can read the chair’s collision properties. It can see what an NPC knows, where a door leads, what state a mission is in, and which objects can interact.

The engine is not just a stage. It is a machine-readable model of a world.

## The world learns how you play

Games already adapt to players. A sports game can watch your first match and recommend a difficulty. A tutorial can teach the controls while learning how quickly you respond.

Now imagine that learning continuing through the whole game.

Does this player explore every side street? Break things when they get frustrated? Avoid fights? Trust strangers? Keep returning to the same character? Those patterns could shape what happens next.

The game could mirror your habits—or challenge them. If you avoid violence, perhaps a mission puts that instinct under pressure. Maybe it offers a way through that the more aggressive player never sees. If you smash everything, maybe the next opportunity depends on restraint.

The game is not assigning you a permanent personality. It is using your history as material.

That is a much richer loop than holding the same carrot in front of every player. The carrot could become a mango, an apple, or a cucumber halfway through the level. The reward, the temptation, even the meaning of the mission could shift in response to what you do.

Two players could begin in the same city, with the same controls and the same basic world, then grow through different stories because the game keeps composing against their choices.

## Generate the next chapter while you are away

The game does not have to invent an entire level in the instant you reach a locked door.

You play for an hour or two, save, and shut down. While you are away, the game looks at what happened and prepares the next stretch: missions, conversations, locations, or consequences shaped around your play. The buildings and characters may be familiar. The storyline may be yours.

By the time you return, the new content is ready.

That shifts generation away from the most demanding moment. The game can compose, check, and save the next chapter between sessions. The studio still designs the world, its rules, assets, and boundaries. The game uses those ingredients to make new arrangements.

This would not make the game literally infinite. It would make its authored outcomes open-ended. The studio would not need to write every path in advance; it would need to build a system capable of making more paths that fit the world.

## The new work of game development

There is a real engineering gap between an AI-generated asset and something that works inside a game. An asset needs to be rigged, animated, collision-safe, consistent, and performant. A generated mission has to be playable. A character needs to remember the right things without becoming incoherent.

That is a substantial problem. But it is a new production requirement, not a reason to assume games must remain fixed catalogues of content.

Game development is changing from making every possible thing to designing how a game can make things. The craft moves into the systems, tools, constraints, and checks that let an agent create without breaking the world.

The exciting question is not whether AI can generate another building or another animation. It is whether the game can understand what the player has done, create a meaningful response, and carry that response forward.

GTA 6 is an astonishingly rich, authored world. The future I’m imagining is a world whose authors build not only its contents, but its capacity to keep making them.

The next open world may not be the one with the biggest map.

It may be the one that keeps writing what happens next.
