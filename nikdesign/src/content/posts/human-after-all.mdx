*Nvidia wouldn't be going through this if its CEO were an AI.*

Just saying.

There is something exquisitely ironic about the company supplying the computational backbone of artificial intelligence finding itself in a crisis over something so spectacularly, embarrassingly human.

Not a software bug. Not a CUDA vulnerability. Not a hallucinating language model.

An affair.

And potentially, according to allegations now under investigation, an intelligence operation.

Jensen Huang has acknowledged infidelity. Nvidia's board has opened an independent investigation into reports of a foreign intelligence effort targeting him. The company says it has no reason to believe its secrets or national security were compromised. Those allegations remain unproven.

Meanwhile, approximately $430 billion in market value disappeared over two trading sessions, according to Reuters. That market movement should not be mistaken for a precisely calculated cost of the controversy: share prices respond to more than one thing at once.

Still. Four hundred and thirty billion dollars.

That's a rather expensive reminder that the most sophisticated hardware on the planet still comes with a human at the top.

And humans, bless our ridiculous little hearts, have a remarkable talent for turning perfectly functioning systems into absolute chaos.

## The most expensive biological processor

For decades, we've been told that artificial intelligence lacks the qualities necessary for leadership.

It doesn't experience emotions. It doesn't understand love. It doesn't possess human intuition, ambition, or desire.

Fair enough.

But apparently, not possessing all of those things might occasionally be a competitive advantage.

An AI CEO wouldn't have an extramarital affair. It wouldn't be seduced over dinner, distracted by romantic attention, or caught trying to explain its personal decisions to a board of directors.

Imagine the quarterly earnings call.

*"Our revenue increased 62%, margins remain healthy, and I am pleased to announce that I have not accidentally introduced a geopolitical security concern through my reproductive instincts."*

Wall Street would be delighted.

Of course, an AI CEO might hallucinate a merger, approve an acquisition based on poisoned data, or confidently bankrupt the company while explaining why everything is going according to plan.

Different bugs. Same potential catastrophe.

And that's where the joke becomes interesting.

## We keep blaming the wrong intelligence

The question has never been whether humans make mistakes. We have several thousand years of documentation confirming that we do.

The question is why we keep designing institutions that allow the mistakes of a single human being to threaten everything around them.

We construct elaborate systems of checks and balances, compliance departments, security protocols, risk assessments, and corporate governance.

Then we elevate one individual to something approaching mythological status.

The visionary. The genius. The indispensable founder.

The man in the leather jacket.

And slowly, the organization becomes indistinguishable from its leader.

His judgment becomes its strategy. His personality becomes its brand. His relationships become its vulnerabilities.

His personal life becomes a potential shareholder concern.

That isn't a failure of intelligence.

**It's a failure of systems design.**

And perhaps the most fascinating part is that this is precisely the kind of problem artificial intelligence ought to help us solve.

Not by replacing every executive with an algorithm wearing a digital tie, but by making leadership less dependent on any single individual's ability to remain consistently brilliant, rational, ethical, and emotionally composed.

A rather ambitious expectation to place on a species that still argues with strangers in YouTube comments.

## Artificial intelligence. Human accountability.

I've spent years exploring a simple proposition: the real opportunity with AI isn't to make machines more human.

It's to make human systems better.

That distinction matters.

We already have plenty of human intelligence. Some of it is remarkable. Some of it elected the people currently making decisions about artificial intelligence.

We don't need machines to inherit every wonderful and terrible characteristic that brought us here.

We need systems capable of remembering what we've forgotten, identifying what we've overlooked, challenging what we've assumed, and occasionally telling us something we would rather not hear.

An AI doesn't need to become the CEO to ask why the CEO is bypassing a security protocol.

It doesn't need emotions to identify a conflict of interest.

It doesn't need ambition to recognize that concentrating extraordinary power in one individual creates extraordinary risk.

And crucially, it shouldn't need permission from the person creating that risk to raise the alarm through an appropriately authorized channel.

But neither should it become an unaccountable surveillance apparatus, policing private relationships or making moral judgments about human beings.

**Privacy isn't a vulnerability. Unmanaged conflicts of interest are.**

A well-designed system must know the difference.

That's the challenge. Not simply creating intelligent machines, but creating an architecture of accountability in which intelligence can challenge authority without quietly becoming authority itself.

Because replacing an occasionally irrational human dictator with an endlessly obedient algorithm is not progress.

And replacing one with an unaccountable artificial dictator is arguably worse.

## The uncomfortable genius of being human

Here's the part that complicates my opening joke.

Nvidia almost certainly wouldn't be Nvidia without Jensen Huang.

His vision, instinct, persistence, appetite for risk, and ability to recognize opportunities before much of the industry understood them helped build one of the most consequential technology companies in history.

Those qualities are human too.

The same species capable of breathtaking stupidity is also capable of extraordinary imagination.

We are inconsistent, emotional, curious, contradictory creatures who somehow learned to turn sand into semiconductors and semiconductors into machines that can reason about our contradictions.

That's rather beautiful, when you think about it.

And deeply absurd.

The goal, then, shouldn't be to eliminate humanity from leadership.

It should be to stop treating human fallibility as an unexpected exception.

Build systems that expect it.

Systems that challenge it.

Systems that remain functional when the person everyone admires turns out to be, well, a person.

Because the ultimate measure of a well-designed organization isn't how brilliantly it performs when its leader gets everything right.

It's how well it survives when its leader gets something spectacularly wrong.

And as artificial intelligence becomes more capable, perhaps the most important question isn't whether a machine deserves to run a company.

Perhaps it's whether any intelligence, artificial or biological, should ever be trusted to run one without meaningful challenge.

After all, we've spent decades teaching machines to recognize patterns.

Maybe it's time we recognized one ourselves.

**Human after all.**

And what a wonderfully inconvenient feature that continues to be.
