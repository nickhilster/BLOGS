When I was a kid growing up in Mumbai, I had a collection of G.I. Joe action figures.

I loved those things.

I'd arrange them at different heights and shoot them with a toy gun. They'd fall, crash, and sometimes lose an arm or a leg.

Naturally, I didn't throw them away.

If one G.I. Joe had a broken leg and another had a broken arm, I'd put together whatever still worked. Frankenstein G.I. Joes.

And those reconstructed characters were always the good guys.

Not because I had developed some philosophy about redemption. I was a kid playing with toys. I just enjoyed making them.

Besides, my G.I. Joes weren't exclusively soldiers.

They were wrestlers.

## The Hitman and the Frankenstein wrestlers

My favourite wrestler was Bret "The Hitman" Hart.

I didn't even know Canada was a country when I first saw him. I saw this guy in pink and black, carrying a beautiful flag, looking cooler than everybody else.

And those sunglasses.

God, those sunglasses were cheesy. You couldn't pay me to wear them today—unless I was going to see Bret Hart. Then I'd wear them proudly.

But Bret knew his audience. He'd walk down to the ring, take off those ridiculous pink shades, and give them to a kid sitting ringside.

Every kid wanted to be that kid.

So my G.I. Joes became wrestlers.

I didn't understand American accents particularly well back then, so I barely followed the cartoon. I didn't even know which characters were supposed to be the heroes or villains until much later.

I just knew Cobra had better costumes and cooler vehicles.

Hasbro supplied the characters, allegiances, and stories. I had the toys. And I had my own stories.

## Everything is LEGO

I'm still doing it.

Today I'm building Codex Sidecar because I want things from Codex that aren't available in the product. I'm building Instinct Sidecar for similar reasons.

I use something, discover its limitations, and wonder what would happen if I connected it to something else.

That doesn't mean the original product is bad. Sometimes it's brilliant. It just isn't the limit of what I can imagine.

I've always been that supposedly lazy person who would rather find a way not to repeat unnecessary work.

But I also enjoy building. I enjoyed reconstructing those G.I. Joes as much as I enjoyed playing with them afterward.

Everything is a set of LEGO to me.

## Why I'm a DJ

Music is another version of the same thing.

A DJ works with recordings made by other people. The rhythms, melodies, vocals, and textures already exist. But the relationships between those recordings don't necessarily exist yet.

Put two tracks together and something changes. Add a third layer. Remove a bassline. Let a percussion loop run underneath something else.

Suddenly you're hearing something that wasn't present in any individual recording.

Sometimes it works beautifully. Sometimes it sounds like absolute garbage. That's part of the fun.

It's why I love mixing across multiple decks. I want to explore what happens *between* tracks.

And the musicians aren't creating from nothing either. They grew up listening to music. Their influences shaped their ears. The same is true of chefs, filmmakers, writers, and inventors.

Even LEGO had predecessors. Nothing comes from nothing. Everything comes from something.

That's not an insult to originality. It's how originality works.

## The bricks and the blueprint

I think about *Fight Club* and *The Matrix* a lot.

In *Fight Club*, the Narrator constructs Tyler Durden, an identity that seems to offer liberation but becomes another form of control.

In *The Matrix*, Agent Smith insists on calling Neo "Mr. Anderson." That's the identity the system recognizes.

Neo refuses it.

"My name is Neo."

And Pink Floyd's *Another Brick in the Wall* gives us the image of children processed into interchangeable units.

These stories aren't identical. But each raises a question about the identities and structures we inherit.

We don't choose all our starting materials. We can, however, question what we're expected to build with them.

**You can inherit the bricks without inheriting the blueprint.**

That doesn't mean every blueprint is wrong. Some preserve generations of hard-won wisdom. It means the blueprint isn't the only possibility.

## Why I call myself a practitioner

Why do doctors *practise* medicine? Why do lawyers practise law? Why do artists and designers speak of a practice?

A doctor with thirty years of experience is still practising—not because they're unqualified, but because knowledge, circumstances, and judgment continue to evolve.

I've described myself as an AI operator. Increasingly, *AI practitioner* feels more accurate.

An operator works with a system. A practitioner investigates what it can do, where it fails, when it shouldn't be used, and how it might change.

If an agent can reliably execute an established workflow, why would I want to keep operating it manually? I'd rather be discovering the next problem, testing the next idea, or improving the system.

The human doesn't disappear from responsibility. The human should be freed from unnecessary repetition.

And practice isn't just disciplined improvement. It's play. Curiosity without a predetermined outcome. Experimenting because something might be interesting.

## Still playing

As a kid, I combined broken action figures and invented wrestling matches.

As a DJ, I combine recordings and discover new musical relationships.

Today, I connect software, models, tools, and workflows to create things I want to exist.

The scale changed. The materials changed. The possibilities changed.

The instinct didn't.

I'm still that kid making Frankenstein G.I. Joes.

**I never stopped playing with LEGO. The bricks just changed.**
