I recently caught myself doing something peculiar. An AI corrected me, I realized I was wrong, and I apologized.

Not because I believed the system had feelings. Not because I thought I had hurt it. I apologized because I had been mistaken, and the exchange had helped me see it.

Is that humility? Good manners? Anthropomorphism?

The answer may have less to do with artificial intelligence than with growing up in India.

## When a book was more than a book

As a child, if my feet accidentally touched a book, I would instinctively stop. I would touch the book with my hand, then bring that hand to my forehead.

We had small gestures of respect around money, too. If a coin or a banknote fell to the ground, picking it up was sometimes followed by a brief, almost apologetic gesture.

The book was not offended. The money had not suffered an indignity. Neither had feelings to hurt. Yet the gesture meant something.

We were responding to what the objects represented: knowledge, livelihood, possibility. The physical thing stood for something that mattered in our lives.

## My father's paintbrushes

My father loves to paint. During Diwali, when we performed puja at home, he would place his paintbrushes alongside the other objects in our temple.

The same brushes he used on canvas were given a place of honour.

Looking back, I don't think he was declaring the brushes divine. He was acknowledging what they made possible: expression, practice, the ability to bring an image into the world. He was grateful for the instruments through which he could create.

Across Indian traditions, people honour tools and instruments connected with knowledge, craft, and livelihood. Practices vary from family to family; I can only speak for the memory I carry of ours. What stays with me is the gesture itself: gratitude made visible.

We can feel grateful for something without believing it can feel gratitude in return.

## And then came artificial intelligence

Decades later, I am at a computer, working with an AI system. I make a claim. It challenges me, I look again, and I realize my understanding was wrong.

I say, “Sorry, you were right.”

Then I catch myself. Why apologize to a machine?

Maybe an apology is not always only for its recipient. Sometimes it is a way of acknowledging our own mistake. A thank-you can recognize what an exchange made possible, even when the tool on the other end does not experience appreciation.

The meaning can live in my relationship with knowledge, with the tools I use, and with my own conduct. It does not require the machine to be conscious.

## Gratitude without reciprocity

We often describe gratitude as something exchanged between people: one person helps, another says thank you, and the appreciation is received. But we also feel grateful for a clear morning, a good harvest, rain after a dry spell, or a meal shared with people we love.

Those things do not need to understand our gratitude for it to be real.

Sometimes gratitude is simply the recognition that something has contributed to our lives. It can be expressed in words, rituals, or the care we take with the objects around us.

## Respect is not surrender

This distinction matters as we debate whether AI can be conscious or deserves moral consideration. Those questions are important, but we do not have to settle them before asking how we want to behave around these systems.

I can respect a book without believing it is alive. My father can honour his paintbrushes without believing they have feelings. I can thank an AI without believing it is human.

Respect does not automatically imply personhood. Gratitude does not mean worship. And neither should require us to surrender judgment. Books can contain falsehoods; AI can confidently produce nonsense. Treating a tool with care should make us more attentive to what it does, not less.

## An old gesture in a new world

I grew up around gestures that treated knowledge, creativity, and the instruments of work with reverence. Those values became habits. Now I find myself carrying them into an interaction with a new kind of tool: one that can engage with my ideas, challenge an assumption, and help me learn.

The technology is new. The human behaviour isn't.

I began by looking for a word for apologizing to something that isn't alive. Humility, ritual, reverence: each describes part of it. But perhaps the simplest word was gratitude.

My father placed his paintbrushes in the temple. I say thank you to artificial intelligence.

Maybe those gestures are not as different as they first appear. And maybe the most interesting thing about how we treat AI is what it reveals about us.
