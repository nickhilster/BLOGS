As evening settles, the first lamps begin to glow.

Small clay **diyas** flicker along windowsills, balconies, garden walls, and temple steps. Doorways bloom with **rangoli**, intricate designs made from coloured powder, rice, flowers, or sand. Kitchens carry the fragrance of cardamom, saffron, roasted nuts, and warm ghee as boxes of **mithai**, or sweets, travel from one household to another.

Outside, streets shimmer beneath strings of lights. Families dress in festive clothing, exchange greetings, and gather for prayer, music, food, and celebration. In some places fireworks crackle overhead; elsewhere temple bells carry through the evening.

This is **Diwali**, also called **Deepavali**, the Festival of Lights.

Diwali is not built around one story or celebrated identically everywhere. Hindus, Jains, and Sikhs connect the season with distinct sacred events, while communities across India and the global diaspora have shaped their own forms of celebration.

What links these traditions is not sameness, but illumination: the belief that darkness, whether understood as injustice, ignorance, despair, or spiritual bondage, does not have the final word.

## Origins and Mythology

The name **Deepavali** comes from Sanskrit words commonly understood to mean a “row of lamps.” The festival falls around the new moon of the Hindu lunar month of Kartika, usually in October or November. Against a moonless sky, every flame becomes especially vivid. [India’s Utsav portal](https://www.utsav.gov.in/major-festival/diwali) offers a concise overview of the festival and its major traditions.

For many Hindus in northern India, Diwali recalls the return of **Rama, Sita, and Lakshmana to Ayodhya**. In the *Ramayana*, Rama is exiled from his kingdom for fourteen years. Sita and Rama’s brother Lakshmana accompany him, and the exile eventually leads to Rama’s battle with Ravana, the king of Lanka who abducts Sita.

A widely told tradition says the people of Ayodhya illuminated the city with lamps when Rama returned. The story gives Diwali its associations with homecoming, family reunited, and **dharma**, moral and cosmic order, prevailing over injustice.

Across much of South India, the festival is strongly connected with **Naraka Chaturdashi** and the defeat of Narakasura. In different tellings, Krishna, sometimes with Satyabhama playing a central role, defeats the tyrant and frees those he has imprisoned. The story emphasizes liberation from cruelty, arrogance, and oppression.

Other Hindu traditions connect the season with **Lakshmi**, Krishna lifting Govardhan Hill, the return of the Pandavas after exile in traditions associated with the *Mahabharata*, or King Bali. Together, they reveal how the festival has grown through many regional and devotional traditions.

For **Jains**, Diwali marks a separate sacred event: **Lord Mahavira’s attainment of nirvana**, final liberation from the cycle of birth and death, at Pawapuri. Lamps represent the continuing light of his teachings. Observances may include prayer, fasting, meditation, temple worship, and reflection on nonviolence and self-discipline.

For **Sikhs**, the period coincides with **Bandi Chhor Divas**, the Day of Liberation. It commemorates the release of **Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji**, the sixth Sikh Guru, from Gwalior Fort. Sikh tradition remembers that the Guru did not accept freedom only for himself; he advocated for other imprisoned rulers and left with fifty-two of them.

Bandi Chhor Divas carries a specifically Sikh message about justice and shared freedom. Gurdwaras are illuminated, **kirtan** is performed, and communities gather for prayer and **langar**, the shared community meal.

## The Five Days of Diwali

Diwali is often described as a five-day festival, although the names, customs, and importance of each day vary by region and community.

### Day One: Dhanteras

**Dhanteras**, or Dhantrayodashi, opens the festive period. Homes and workplaces are cleaned, lamps are lit, and many families purchase something associated with good fortune, traditionally metal utensils, silver, or gold. The day may honour Lakshmi, Kubera, and Dhanvantari, the divine physician associated with Ayurveda.

### Day Two: Naraka Chaturdashi

The second day is known as **Naraka Chaturdashi**, Chhoti Diwali, or Kali Chaudas in different places. In South Indian traditions it celebrates Narakasura’s defeat. Some households begin before sunrise with an oil bath, new clothing, sweets, prayers, and lamps, making the morning itself the heart of the celebration.

### Day Three: Diwali and Lakshmi Puja

The third day is commonly the principal night of Diwali. Families illuminate their homes, create rangoli, exchange sweets and gifts, and perform **Lakshmi Puja**. Ganesha is also worshipped in many households, with prayers for wisdom, auspicious beginnings, stability, and prosperity.

### Day Four: Govardhan Puja, Annakut, or Bali Pratipada

The fourth day has different meanings across India. Many Vaishnava communities observe **Govardhan Puja**, recalling Krishna protecting the people of Braj by lifting Govardhan Hill. Temples and homes may prepare **Annakut**, a “mountain of food” offered in gratitude.

Elsewhere the day is celebrated as Bali Pratipada or Padwa and may be associated with King Bali, marriage, family relationships, or the beginning of a new year.

### Day Five: Bhai Dooj

The festival concludes with **Bhai Dooj**, also called Bhau Beej or Bhai Phota. The day honours sibling bonds. Sisters traditionally bless their brothers’ well-being, while brothers offer gifts and affirm their care. Many contemporary families interpret it more broadly as a celebration of mutual affection, responsibility, and protection among siblings.

## Regional and Diaspora Variations

There is no single standardized Indian Diwali.

In parts of North India, Rama’s return and evening Lakshmi Puja are central. In Gujarat, Diwali is closely connected with Lakshmi, commerce, the closing of old financial books, and the Gujarati New Year. In West Bengal, Odisha, and Assam, the main night often coincides with **Kali Puja**, dedicated to the goddess Kali.

In Maharashtra, the festive cycle may include **Faral**, an assortment of homemade sweet and savoury foods shared with guests. In Goa and parts of South India, large representations of Narakasura may be created and destroyed, while early-morning bathing and celebration can take precedence over the primarily evening customs familiar in the north.

Diaspora communities in Canada, the United States, the United Kingdom, East Africa, Southeast Asia, the Caribbean, Fiji, Mauritius, and elsewhere continue these traditions while adapting them to new surroundings. Celebrations take place in homes, temples, gurdwaras, Jain centres, schools, workplaces, museums, and city halls.

Public festivals bring together music, dance, food, storytelling, crafts, and ceremonial lamp-lighting. For younger generations, they can be acts of cultural continuity: a way to inherit tradition while building belonging in the countries they call home.

## Modern Celebration

Today, Diwali moves easily between the intimate and the spectacular.

At home, people clean and decorate, prepare family recipes, wear festive clothing, visit relatives, exchange gifts, and offer prayers. Families separated by distance may light lamps together over video calls.

Businesses may open new account books, communities organize charitable initiatives, and places of worship distribute meals. Prosperity is increasingly understood not only as personal wealth, but also as health, generosity, security, and shared well-being.

Fireworks remain popular, but their effects on air quality, noise, animals, and health have prompted reflection. They are not the only cause of seasonal pollution in Indian cities, where traffic, industry, weather, and agricultural burning also matter. Still, many families now choose quieter celebrations.

Alternatives include community light installations, laser displays, reusable diyas, natural rangoli materials, limited fireworks, or simply gathering beneath lamps and lanterns. These changes need not diminish Diwali’s meaning. They can return attention to its most enduring symbol: light itself.

## A Light That Is Shared

Diwali cannot be reduced to one legend, ritual, or religious interpretation.

For one family, it evokes Rama’s return to Ayodhya. For another, it recalls Krishna’s victory over Narakasura. For Jains, it marks Mahavira’s liberation. For Sikhs, Bandi Chhor Divas honours Guru Hargobind Sahib Ji and the principle that freedom should be shared.

A lamp is small. It does not erase the night or pretend darkness never existed. It creates enough light for people to see one another, gather together, and find the next step forward.

Diwali celebrates victory, but also renewal; prosperity, but also generosity; homecoming, but also liberation. In every diya placed at a doorway lives an old hope: that wisdom can overcome ignorance, compassion can overcome isolation, and darkness can be answered by light.
