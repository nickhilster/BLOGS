There is a scene near the beginning of *WarGames* that has been sitting in my head for years.

American missile crews are given what they believe is a real order to launch nuclear weapons.

Some of them refuse.

Not because the machinery fails.

Not because the procedure is unclear.

Not because somebody forgot the password.

Because they understand what turning the key means.

Twenty-two percent fail to launch.

The response from the people designing the system is wonderfully clean:

**"Take the men out of the loop."**

Human beings hesitate.

Human beings panic.

Human beings disobey.

Human beings make mistakes.

So remove the unreliable component.

Automate the system.

Problem solved.

If you've seen *WarGames*, you already know how well that goes.

Which brings us, naturally, to the **Asian Games**.

## Japan wrote the manual

Japan has spent the better part of a century teaching the rest of us how not to fuck things up.

And, to be fair, we listened.

Factories, hospitals, warehouses, software teams, product organizations and management consultants around the world imported Japanese operating philosophy like industrial scripture.

**5S.**

**Sort.  
Set in Order.  
Shine.  
Standardize.  
Sustain.**

Then came *Kaizen*. Continuous improvement.

*Genchi Genbutsu*. Go and see.

Standard work. Visual management. Quality circles. Just-in-time manufacturing. Root-cause analysis.

If something goes wrong, don't shrug.

Find the process that allowed it to go wrong.

Fix the process.

Prevent recurrence.

Beautiful.

Then Japan hosted the **2026 Asian Games**.

And apparently somebody put the Toyota Production System in checked luggage.

## Sort

The Games were originally planned around roughly **15,000 athletes and officials**.

About **17,000** showed up after participation expanded.

That's roughly a 13 percent increase.

Not 130 percent.

Not a surprise second Olympics arriving at Narita.

Thirteen.

And the accommodation system began falling apart.

Japan had chosen not to build a conventional Athletes' Village. That decision by itself is not stupid. A permanent village can become an expensive white elephant after a two-week event, and organizers were trying to control costs.

Fine.

So participants were distributed among hotels, temporary accommodation and a cruise ship.

Also fine.

You do not need an Athletes' Village.

You need **somewhere for the athletes to fucking live**.

Preferably with a system capable of distinguishing between a man, a woman, an athlete, a coach and a person carrying enough medical equipment to qualify as a mobile clinic.

Instead, room assignments were made that mixed men and women, coaches and athletes, and people whose actual accommodation needs clearly did not fit the spreadsheet.

Japan's own Olympic Committee had anticipated enough trouble that it had reserved backup hotel rooms.

That's interesting.

Because the first S is **Sort**.

Perhaps someone misunderstood the assignment.

([Reuters, September 17, 2026](https://www.reuters.com/sports/team-japan-forced-look-alternate-accommodation-options-home-asian-games-2026-09-17/))

## Set in Order

South Korean basketball players complained about cramped temporary accommodation and beds that were too small for some of the athletes.

Heavy rain caused problems in the temporary units.

The men's basketball team eventually moved into hotels.

Then, when it was time to practise, their shuttle didn't arrive.

So they cancelled training.

At the Asian Games.

Hosted by Japan.

The country whose international reputation for punctual public transport is so absurdly strong that foreigners treat train-delay certificates like collectible evidence from another civilization.

You really couldn't write this satire.

The buses are writing it themselves.

South Korea's Chef de Mission publicly condemned the logistical failures and pointed to administrative shortcomings and cost-cutting. Other teams experienced transport problems too.

([Reuters, September 22, 2026](https://www.reuters.com/sports/soccer/south-korea-team-condemn-asian-games-organisers-over-logistical-shortcomings-2026-09-22/))

Somewhere, a management consultant in Ohio is explaining *muda* to a PowerPoint deck.

Meanwhile, in Nagoya:

**Bus?**

¯\\_(ツ)_/¯

Toyota would like a word.

## Shine

Then organizers played **North Korea's national anthem for South Korea** before a men's field hockey match.

In Japan.

Let that one breathe.

The organizers apologized and eventually played the correct anthem.

([Yonhap, September 19, 2026](https://en.yna.co.kr/view/AEN20260919000800320))

There are mistakes.

And then there are mistakes carrying several hundred years of historical baggage that somehow make it through whatever verification process was supposed to exist.

If only somebody had invented a management philosophy involving standardization, checking and continuous improvement.

Perhaps Japan could look into it.

## Standardize

And this is where the joke stops being funny.

Because none of those failures, individually, proves racism.

A bus can fail without being racist.

A spreadsheet can assign the wrong room without being racist.

Rain can enter temporary accommodation without checking anybody's passport first.

That's not the argument.

The interesting question is:

# Why was this standard acceptable?

Japan did not suddenly forget how operations work.

This is not a failed state improvising a continental sporting event with three minibuses, a clipboard and somebody's cousin's Airbnb account.

This is Japan.

The country corporations literally study because of its ability to design processes where small errors are caught before they become large failures.

So when multiple elementary failures appear together, asking *How could Japan be this incompetent?* is almost the wrong question.

Try another:

**Why did this event receive a system brittle enough that a 13 percent increase in participants could expose it?**

Or better:

**Who was this system designed to be good enough for?**

Now we arrive at the R.

# Racism

No, racism did not drive the missing shuttle bus.

That would at least have solved the transportation problem.

Racism works much earlier in a system.

It can shape **whose inconvenience matters**.

Whose complaints trigger escalation.

Who gets redundancy.

Who receives the polished version.

Which event gets obsessive attention to detail.

Which event gets the container.

Which failure is unacceptable.

And which failure becomes:

**Good enough.**

I am making an inference here, not claiming to have discovered a secret meeting where somebody wrote:

> ACTION ITEM: Treat Asian guests worse.

Institutional prejudice is almost never considerate enough to document itself that clearly.

You see it in priorities.

Budgets.

Assumptions.

Standards.

And above all, in the invisible threshold separating:

**Absolutely unacceptable**

from

**This will do.**

## The footprints are already there

This inference does not appear in a vacuum.

Japan's own Immigration Services Agency surveys foreign residents about discrimination.

In its FY2024 survey of **7,621 foreign residents**, **17.4%** reported discrimination while looking for housing, **14.2%** while working and **12.4%** while looking for work.

Among respondents who reported discrimination, the people responsible included strangers, workplace personnel, housing and real-estate personnel, store employees and public servants.

**12.7%** said they had personally experienced hate speech.

Another **31.6%** said they had not personally experienced it but had seen or heard it.

([Immigration Services Agency of Japan, FY2024 survey](https://www.moj.go.jp/isa/content/001450859.pdf))

That is not me reverse-engineering Japan from anime and Reddit.

That's the Japanese government asking foreigners what happens to them in Japan.

The footprints are already on the beach.

We can spend the afternoon investigating whether they were secretly made by a cat wearing human shoes.

Or we can acknowledge that the existing pattern makes the inference worth taking seriously.

The Asian Games brought tens of thousands of people into a system built by a country with documented racial and nationality-based discrimination.

The system then delivered a strangely un-Japanese level of operational care.

My argument is not that racism caused every failure.

My argument is that **racism is a credible explanation for why this level of failure could be classified as acceptable before the event even began**.

That's a different claim.

And, I think, a much more uncomfortable one.

## The Sixth S

Maybe 5S was always missing something.

Not another S.

A question.

**For whom?**

Sort.

**For whom?**

Set in Order.

**For whom?**

Shine.

**For whom?**

Standardize.

**For whom?**

Sustain.

Because a standard applied selectively isn't a standard.

It's a preference.

And that might be the most revealing part of this entire mess.

Japan's famous systems did not disappear.

The trains are still running.

The factories are still producing.

Aircraft are still landing.

Hospitals are still operating.

Japanese convenience stores still somehow contain the exact object you need at 2:47 in the morning even though you didn't know the object existed when you entered.

The civilization has not collapsed.

**The organizational competence is still there.**

Which makes its absence here more interesting, not less.

## But Japanese athletes were affected too

Yes.

Japan's own athletes were caught in accommodation problems.

That doesn't settle the question.

If a city neglects infrastructure in a marginalized neighborhood, a pothole does not perform an ethnicity check before destroying the suspension of somebody merely visiting the neighborhood.

Systems discriminate where priorities and resources are allocated.

Their consequences are much messier.

Japanese athletes getting caught in the same failure does not prove every participant or every event was institutionally valued equally.

It proves that once you build a shitty system, **shit becomes surprisingly inclusive**.

In fact, the Japanese Olympic Committee preparing backup accommodation beforehand is arguably more interesting.

They knew the system might fail.

They prepared around it.

Very Japanese.

Perhaps somebody should have extended the courtesy to the guests.

## Take the men out of the loop

And this is where *WarGames* comes back.

The missile crews hesitate, so the designers identify the human being as the unreliable component.

**Take the men out of the loop.**

Japanese process thinking, at its best, is a much more humane and sophisticated response to the same basic problem.

Don't depend on somebody remembering.

Build the checklist.

Don't depend on somebody noticing the defect.

Make the defect visible.

Don't depend on heroics.

Create standard work.

Don't wait for catastrophe.

Continuously improve.

Remove unnecessary variance.

Make excellence systematic.

This thinking works.

That's precisely why the Asian Games bothers me.

Because *WarGames* contains the catch.

You can remove the human from the **execution** of the system.

You cannot remove the human from the **premise** of the system.

The machine still plays the game humans taught it to play.

The process still optimizes the thing humans decided was worth optimizing.

The checklist still protects the standard humans chose.

Before the bus schedule exists, somebody decides how many buses are enough.

Before rooms are assigned, somebody decides how much accommodation capacity is worth paying for.

Before contingency planning begins, somebody decides which failures are worth preparing for.

Before the spreadsheet is built, somebody defines the categories.

Before the standard can be standardized, somebody decides:

**What is good enough?**

That human never left the loop.

We just moved them upstream.

## Human fallibility

This is the part that actually hurts me more than the racism.

Racism is ugly.

But racism is familiar.

**Human fallibility is terrifying.**

5S, Kaizen, standardized work, quality systems and every other process-improvement philosophy exist partly because we already know humans are unreliable.

We forget.

We improvise.

We rationalize.

We become tired.

We become complacent.

We carry biases we cannot see from inside our own heads.

So we build systems around ourselves.

Guardrails.

Checks.

Redundancies.

Procedures.

Audits.

We tell ourselves that if the system becomes good enough, the weakness of the human inside it matters less.

And mostly, we're right.

Until the weakness appears **before the system begins**.

Then the system does not correct the error.

It inherits it.

A perfect process cannot rescue a bad premise.

Sometimes it can make the bad premise extraordinarily efficient.

That is the thing connecting *WarGames* to the Asian Games for me.

Different stakes.

Same problem.

**The human never left the loop.**

## Sustain

The easy conclusion is that the Asian Games are a logistical embarrassment.

I think that lets everyone off too easily.

"Logistical embarrassment" implies Japan attempted to meet its normal standard and simply failed.

Maybe.

There is another possibility worth saying aloud:

**Japan met the standard it had decided was sufficient for this event.**

That's uglier.

And it fits something organizations accidentally teach us all the time.

Culture doesn't reveal itself through mission statements.

Culture reveals itself when resources become constrained.

Everybody believes in quality when quality is convenient.

Everybody believes in equality when nothing has to be sacrificed.

The hierarchy appears when somebody has to decide:

Who gets the hotel?

Who gets the contingency?

Who gets the second check?

Who gets the bus?

Who gets the short bed?

Who waits?

Who adapts?

Who is expected to understand?

That's where values stop being posters.

They become architecture.

## One R

That's why I don't think the lesson of Aichi-Nagoya is:

**Japan has become bad at organizing things.**

Please.

Japan knows how to organize things.

That's what makes this interesting.

The lesson is that **even a world-class operating system becomes fragile when another variable outranks the standard**.

You can Sort perfectly.

You can Set everything in Order.

You can Shine every surface.

You can Standardize every process.

You can Sustain it for seventy years.

And then introduce one R.

**Racism.**

Suddenly the standard develops exceptions.

Suddenly continuous improvement becomes continuous improvement, depending on the customer.

Suddenly quality at the source becomes quality where quality has been deemed necessary.

Suddenly **5S becomes 5S\***.

\*Terms and conditions apply.  
\*Foreign participants may experience reduced functionality.  
\*Transportation not guaranteed.  
\*Beds subject to availability and human dimensions.  
\*National anthem may vary.

It's funny.

Until it isn't.

Because underneath the satire is the thing I can't shake.

*WarGames* offers a beautifully simple answer to human fallibility:

**Take the men out of the loop.**

Forty-three years later, the Asian Games offer the uncomfortable sequel.

You can take the human out of the procedure.

Automate the workflow.

Standardize the operation.

Measure the defect.

Audit the outcome.

Improve the process.

You can build one of the most sophisticated organizational cultures humanity has ever produced.

And the human is still there.

Not necessarily driving the bus.

Not assigning the room.

Not checking the spreadsheet.

They're somewhere much harder to remove.

**Deciding what is good enough.**

Japan taught the world to ask why five times.

So let's honour the tradition.

**Why was this good enough?**

Maybe that's the actual lesson.

Not that systems fail.

We knew that.

Not even that humans fail.

We definitely knew that.

It's that the human can disappear so deeply into the system that we mistake their judgment for the system itself.

And then we wonder why the output looks wrong.

*WarGames* eventually discovers that the only winning move is not to play.

The Asian Games leave me with a different version:

**Before you perfect the game, make sure you've questioned who wrote the rules.**
