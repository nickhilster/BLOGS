Two of the greatest love stories ever told came out in 1999.

One was about a man who couldn't sleep and started beating the shit out of strangers in a basement.

The other was about a computer programmer who discovered that reality wasn't real.

Neither was marketed as a love story.

That's probably why they work.

*Fight Club* is, for me, the greatest romantic love story ever told.

*The Matrix* is the greatest story of love ever told.

Those sound like the same statement.

They're not.

## I Am Jack's Love Story

People don't usually describe *Fight Club* as romantic.

It's a film about masculinity, consumerism, alienation, violence, identity, capitalism, nihilism and the strange things that happen when men who have been taught not to feel anything discover that getting punched in the face makes them feel alive.

And then there's Marla Singer.

Marla is there almost from the beginning.

The Narrator hates her.

Of course he does.

She has invaded his support groups, the places where he goes to pretend to be dying so that he can finally cry. Her presence destroys the lie because he recognizes another liar.

But there's something else about Marla that bothers him.

She sees him.

And being seen is dangerous when you've constructed your entire life around not having to look at yourself.

Then Tyler Durden arrives.

Tyler is everything the Narrator isn't.

Tyler doesn't care what you own. Tyler doesn't care what your boss thinks. Tyler fucks. Tyler fights. Tyler makes things. Tyler destroys things. Tyler walks into a room as though nobody has ever taught him to ask permission to exist.

The Narrator falls completely under his spell.

And somewhere in the middle of all this, Tyler starts sleeping with Marla.

That's where *Fight Club* becomes a very strange love triangle.

Because there aren't three people in it.

There are two.

The Narrator wants Marla, but Tyler can have her.

The Narrator wants freedom, but Tyler can live it.

The Narrator wants confidence, sexuality, danger, purpose, rebellion.

Tyler gets to be all of those things for him.

Tyler is the man the Narrator has fallen in love with becoming.

And that's what makes the ending so much more than a clever psychological reveal.

The Narrator cannot simply defeat Tyler.

He has to accept that Tyler is him.

And then he has to let Tyler die.

That's the price of becoming himself.

He puts the gun in his mouth and pulls the trigger.

Tyler disappears.

And then Marla arrives.

Not for Tyler.

For him.

He takes her hand.

Outside the window, the buildings begin to fall.

And he says:

> “You met me at a very strange time in my life.”

It's one of my favourite romantic endings because almost everything we normally associate with romance has been stripped away.

There is no grand declaration.

No wedding.

No kiss in the rain.

The world is quite literally collapsing outside the window.

There are just two profoundly strange people holding hands.

And for the first time in the movie, the man holding Marla's hand is actually himself.

That's the love story.

Not that Marla saved him.

Not that Tyler saved him.

He finally became capable of standing beside another person without needing somebody else to be him.

## There Is No Spoon

Then there is *The Matrix*.

Also 1999.

Also about a man who knows something is wrong with his life but cannot explain what.

Also about meeting someone who tells him that the person he thinks he is isn't really him.

But where *Fight Club* tears its protagonist apart, *The Matrix* asks him to wake up.

And at the centre of that awakening is Morpheus.

Morpheus believes in Neo before Neo gives him any reason to.

That's important.

Neo doesn't begin the movie as The One.

He begins as Thomas Anderson.

Confused. Searching. Frightened.

Morpheus looks at that man and sees somebody else.

Not because Neo has proven himself.

Because Morpheus believes.

And belief is one of the strangest forms love can take.

To love somebody for who they are is one thing.

To see who somebody might become—and give them the freedom to discover it themselves—is something else entirely.

Morpheus doesn't make Neo The One.

He can't.

He can show him the door.

He can tell him the truth.

He can train him.

He can risk his life for him.

But Neo has to walk through it.

Even the Oracle refuses to simply hand Neo an identity.

The entire film keeps returning the responsibility to him.

Who are you?

What do you believe?

What are you willing to do?

And then Morpheus is captured.

Neo has every rational reason to leave him behind.

Instead, Neo goes back.

That's when something changes.

The student who was saved becomes the person doing the saving.

Morpheus believed Neo could become something before Neo believed it himself.

Now Neo's actions begin catching up with Morpheus's faith.

Then Trinity does something similar.

Neo dies.

And Trinity refuses the conclusion.

She tells him that the Oracle said she would fall in love with The One.

And she loves him.

Therefore he cannot be dead.

It's wonderfully irrational.

It's also perfectly consistent with everything *The Matrix* has been telling us.

The rules you have accepted are not necessarily the limits of what is possible.

Neo gets up.

And finally he sees the Matrix for what it is.

## Two Men Wake Up

That's why these two movies belong together for me.

*Fight Club* and *The Matrix* appear to be completely different stories.

One is filthy, physical, nihilistic and deliberately ugly.

The other is philosophical, technological, mythological and almost religious.

But look at the men at their centres.

Both begin asleep.

Not literally.

They are asleep inside identities they inherited.

One has an apartment filled with furniture that is supposed to tell him who he is.

The other has an office cubicle and a name that is supposed to tell him who he is.

Neither believes the life in front of him can possibly be all there is.

Then someone appears.

Tyler Durden.

Morpheus.

Follow me.

The world isn't what you think it is.

You aren't who you think you are.

There is another way to live.

And both protagonists follow.

But here's where the stories separate.

Tyler represents the self the Narrator thinks he needs to become.

Morpheus sees the self Neo might discover.

Tyler says, in effect:

*Become me.*

Morpheus says:

*Become you.*

That difference is enormous.

One relationship eventually has to be destroyed.

The other has to be transcended.

The Narrator has to kill Tyler because as long as Tyler exists separately from him, he hasn't actually become whole.

Neo has to outgrow his dependence on Morpheus's belief because eventually Morpheus cannot be the reason Neo believes.

He has to know.

And both transformations happen because of love.

## Romantic Love and the Love That Sets You Free

That's why I make a distinction between these films.

*Fight Club* is my greatest **romantic love story**.

Because underneath all the testosterone, blood, soap, pornography, terrorism and IKEA furniture is a story about intimacy.

A man who cannot tolerate himself cannot properly love another person.

So he invents someone he can admire.

He lets that person live for him.

And eventually he has to destroy the fantasy, accept himself and reach for Marla with his own hand.

*The Matrix* is my greatest **story of love**.

Because its idea of love is larger.

Morpheus believes.

Trinity recognizes.

Neo chooses.

Again and again, somebody sees something in another person that they cannot yet see in themselves.

But nobody gets to make the final choice for them.

That's what makes Morpheus's love so powerful to me.

He doesn't ask Neo to become Morpheus.

He doesn't ask Neo to worship him.

He doesn't even demand that Neo believe what Morpheus believes.

He shows him the truth and lets him choose.

That's love without possession.

Love without ownership.

Love as faith in another person's capacity to become themselves.

## 1999

And somehow, both movies arrived in the same year.

1999.

Right at the end of one century and the beginning of another.

Two movies about men staring at lives that are supposedly successful and realizing something is terribly wrong.

Two movies about waking up.

Two movies about rejecting identities manufactured for you.

Two movies about discovering that freedom is much more frightening than captivity because once someone shows you the door, you have to decide whether to walk through it.

One gives us Tyler Durden.

The other gives us Morpheus.

One man says:

Destroy everything that made you this person.

The other says:

I can only show you the door.

And somehow both stories eventually arrive at love.

Not the decorative kind.

Not love as a subplot added because the protagonist needs somebody to kiss before the credits.

Love as transformation.

Love as recognition.

Love as the terrifying experience of being seen.

Love as believing in somebody.

Love as letting go of the person you thought you needed to be.

Love as becoming capable of standing beside another human being as yourself.

Maybe that's why neither movie needed to call itself a love story.

The best ones rarely do.

*Fight Club* ends with two people holding hands while the world they knew collapses around them.

*The Matrix* ends with a man finally seeing the world as it really is.

One is the greatest romantic love story ever told.

The other is the greatest story of love ever told.

At least to me.

And both are really asking the same question:

**What happens when someone finally gives you permission to become yourself?**
