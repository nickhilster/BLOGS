Lately I have been watching a strange kind of footage in the background while I work.

What caught my attention was not the dramatic part. I kept skipping that.

I wanted the conversations before it.

Then I wanted the conversations after it.

Eventually I realized why.

I spend a lot of my life trying to understand how people behave so I can design things for them. And here was something unusually difficult to find in modern media: people responding to changing circumstances before anyone involved knows how the story ends.

That is much more interesting to me than the ending.

## We Say We Design for Humans

Designers spend a lot of time trying to understand people.

We interview them. We observe them. We run usability tests. We build personas and journey maps. We study analytics. We ask people what they want, what frustrates them, what they would do, and why they did something.

All of this is useful.

All of it also has a problem.

The moment I sit someone down and say, “I’m conducting research,” I have changed the situation.

They know they are being studied.

They know there is a designer sitting across from them.

They know we are interested in what they have to say.

Put someone into a usability test and now they are not simply using your product.

They are using your product **while knowing somebody is watching them use your product.**

That does not invalidate research.

It just means we should never confuse research conditions with life.

Humans are messier than our research sessions.

## Most Observation Changes the Thing Being Observed

This problem is not unique to design.

Most human behaviour we encounter through media has already been framed.

Movies and television are constructed.

Documentaries have filmmakers, subjects and narratives.

Social media is performance almost by definition.

Reality television might be the funniest example. Put people into an artificial environment surrounded by cameras, producers and incentives to remain interesting, and then call the result reality.

What fascinated me about the footage I had started watching was that the camera existed for another reason.

The interaction was not staged for me.

That does not make the footage objective. The situations themselves are highly selective, and someone still chooses what eventually reaches an audience.

There are filters everywhere.

But underneath those filters is something I rarely get to observe for long stretches:

**people dealing with a situation before they know what the story is.**

For someone interested in human behaviour, that is gold.

## Everyone Has a Different Model

Once I started paying attention, I stopped focusing on one person.

Every new person entering a situation brought a different amount of information.

One person knows why the interaction began but little else.

Another knows things nobody has asked about yet.

Someone arrives halfway through and has to rapidly construct an understanding from whatever they can observe and whatever other people tell them.

Everybody is simultaneously observing everybody else.

Everyone has a model of what is happening.

Those models are rarely identical.

Then new information arrives.

Watch what happens.

## The Research Stage

A developing interaction can look remarkably like research.

Start with an observation.

Ask a question.

Form a hypothesis.

Look for evidence.

Ask another question.

Notice a contradiction.

Update the hypothesis.

Check another source.

Update again.

Eventually:

Act.

**Observe → Question → Hypothesize → Test → Update → Decide → Act.**

Sound familiar?

The interesting complication is that the person being observed is observing right back.

What does this person know?

Why did they ask that?

What did somebody else tell them?

What happens next?

The observer changes the observed.

The observed changes the observer.

And both keep updating.

That is much closer to real human systems than the clean separation between “researcher” and “participant” that our diagrams sometimes suggest.

## Watch the Model Break

This might be the most useful part for me as a designer.

Someone forms an explanation of what is happening.

Then reality contradicts it.

What happens next?

Some people update immediately.

Some resist.

Some reinterpret the new information so they can preserve their original belief.

Some become defensive.

Some become curious.

Some double down.

The interesting question is not whether the original assumption was correct.

Nobody gets perfect information.

The interesting question is:

**How quickly can you recognize that your model no longer matches reality?**

That is design.

We make models constantly.

We think we know what the problem is.

We think we know who the user is.

We think we know why they are struggling.

We think we know what feature they need.

Then we put something in front of an actual human being.

Reality responds.

Sometimes designers update the model.

Sometimes we explain reality away.

“The user did not understand the prototype.”

“They are not our target customer.”

“They were not using it properly.”

Maybe.

Or maybe the model was wrong.

## Humans Change When Conditions Change

This is another thing that becomes obvious when you watch the same interaction evolve over time.

A person can begin confident.

New information arrives.

Now they are defensive.

Then frustrated.

Then negotiating.

Then calm.

Then surprisingly cooperative.

It would be easy to ask which one is the “real” person.

I think that is the wrong question.

They are all the person.

The environment changed.

The information changed.

The incentives changed.

The perceived options changed.

So behaviour changed.

That matters when designing for humans.

We love turning behaviour into attributes.

This user is impatient.

That user is risk-averse.

This person does not understand technology.

That customer values convenience.

Maybe.

But behaviour does not emerge from a person alone.

It emerges from a person **inside a situation**.

Change the situation and you may change the behaviour.

Which means sometimes the thing we should be designing is not the person's next action.

It is the conditions surrounding that action.

## The Plan Lasts Until Reality Shows Up

Everyone starts with some version of a plan.

Then reality happens.

A routine interaction develops in an unexpected direction.

New information appears.

Another person enters.

An assumption stops making sense.

Five minutes later, the original plan is almost irrelevant.

Nobody could have designed the entire sequence beforehand.

There can be procedures, training and constraints.

But within those boundaries, people still have to improvise.

That is an important distinction.

**Improvisation is not the absence of a system.**

It is what happens when a system encounters a reality it could not completely predict.

We do this badly in design sometimes.

We try to anticipate every path.

Every state.

Every edge case.

Every possible thing a human might do.

Eventually reality invents another one.

Maybe resilient design is not about predicting everything.

Maybe it is about building systems that can remain useful when our predictions fail.

## Before We Know the Ending

The footage I have been watching is not some magical source of truth about human beings.

It is too selective for that.

But it has reminded me of something I do not want to forget as a designer.

**Humans do not experience our designs as journeys on a whiteboard.**

They arrive carrying information we do not have.

They misunderstand things.

We misunderstand them.

Their priorities change.

Their environment changes.

They make decisions that look irrational until you understand what they believed at the moment they made them.

They improvise.

So do we.

And unlike the researcher looking back at the session, the person living through it does not know what is coming next.

That is probably why I keep watching.

I am interested in the moment before everything makes sense.

When several people have incomplete models of reality.

When new information is still arriving.

When the plan has stopped working.

And everyone has to decide what to do next.

**That is what happens when you design for humans.**
