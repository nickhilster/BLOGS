Recently, I started reading more about the early history of ISKCON in America. I expected it to be a fairly straightforward story: an Indian spiritual teacher arrives in New York in the 1960s, gathers a small group of followers, and over time that becomes what much of the world comes to know as the Hare Krishna movement.

But the more I read, the more familiar the surrounding history started to feel. The same names and ideas kept appearing around it: psychedelics, the counterculture, [Aldous Huxley](https://www.huxley.net/), [Alan Watts](https://alanwatts.org/biography), [Albert Hofmann](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Hofmann), questions about consciousness, Indian philosophy, and eventually Krishna. What surprised me was not that these things could be connected after the fact, but that many of them had already crossed paths historically.

That mattered to me personally because I had encountered several of these ideas years apart, through completely different doorways. I had never thought of them as one story. Then history started showing me that the rooms were connected before I ever walked into them.

## The story did not start in the 1960s

Long before LSD, Haight-Ashbury or the Beatles, an Indian monk stood before an American audience in Chicago. On September 11, 1893, [Swami Vivekananda](https://parliamentofreligions.org/1893-chicago/vivekanandas-speech-to-1893-parliament/) addressed the World's Parliament of Religions and began with the now-famous words, “Sisters and Brothers of America.”

Vivekananda was not bringing ISKCON or Krishna consciousness to America. His philosophical tradition and emphasis were different, but he was doing something historically significant: presenting Hindu philosophy and Vedanta directly to a major Western audience. His appearance became one of the landmark moments in the public introduction of Hindu thought to America.

That matters because it changes the timeline. Indian spirituality did not suddenly arrive in the West alongside hippies in the 1960s. There had already been decades of transmission. In 1920, [Paramahansa Yogananda](https://yogananda.org/a-beloved-world-teacher) arrived in the United States and went on to teach meditation and Kriya Yoga to Western audiences. By the time the psychedelic era arrived, Indian spiritual thought was not entering completely unfamiliar territory. There had already been decades of exposure, translation and curiosity.

## A chemical doorway

[Albert Hofmann](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Albert-Hofmann) first synthesized LSD-25 in 1938 while working at Sandoz and discovered its psychedelic effects in 1943. He would later write about that discovery, and his increasingly complicated relationship with what happened to it culturally, in [*LSD: My Problem Child*](https://archive.org/details/lsdmyproblemchil0000hofm_o9o4).

The discovery itself did not immediately create a counterculture, but it introduced something unusual into the Western conversation about consciousness. A chemical substance could radically alter perception, identity, time, meaning and the sense of self. The philosophical implications were enormous.

A decade later, [Aldous Huxley](https://www.huxley.net/) took mescaline and wrote [*The Doors of Perception*](https://www.huxley.net/doors-of-perception/aldoushuxley-thedoorsofperception.pdf). That book matters here because Huxley was not simply documenting a strange drug experience. He was asking whether ordinary consciousness might be a filtered version of something much larger, and whether altered states could expose aspects of perception normally unavailable to us.

The title came from [William Blake](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poets/william-blake), and the phrase later gave Jim Morrison's band [The Doors](https://thedoors.com/) its name. Even that small detail says something about how quickly the language of altered perception moved from poetry to philosophy, from philosophy to psychedelic culture, and from psychedelic culture into rock music.

By the 1960s, the question was no longer confined to writers and researchers. A generation of people was experimenting directly with altered states of consciousness. That does not mean those experiences proved any particular spiritual worldview. A feeling of unity does not automatically prove Vedanta, ego dissolution does not prove Buddhism, and a mystical experience does not prove Krishna. The interpretation still comes afterward.

But once someone has had an experience in which the ordinary sense of self changes dramatically, questions about consciousness stop being purely academic. What exactly is consciousness? Am I really identical to this ordinary sense of “me”? Why can that identity disappear? Why can time seem to disappear? Why can separation between self and world suddenly feel artificial? Those are not small questions, and suddenly an entire generation was asking them.

## After the door opens

One of the most interesting figures standing between Eastern philosophy and the psychedelic counterculture was [Alan Watts](https://alanwatts.org/biography). Watts had spent years interpreting Zen, Taoism and other Asian philosophical traditions for Western audiences, and he also took psychedelics seriously enough to write explicitly about their relationship to religious experience.

But Watts also recognized a problem: the extraordinary experience could itself become another attachment. His famous metaphor, “Once you get the message, hang up the phone,” captured that tension neatly. The point was not necessarily that the experience was false; it was that endlessly reproducing the experience was not the same thing as understanding what it meant.

Huxley had asked what might lie beyond the ordinary doors of perception. Watts was asking what you were supposed to do once those doors had opened, while Hofmann, from another angle, was wrestling with what happens when a powerful tool leaves a careful context and becomes a mass cultural phenomenon.

Then came [Timothy Leary](https://archives.nypl.org/mss/18400), who pushed the psychedelic movement much further into public life. “Turn on, tune in, drop out” represented something very different from Hofmann's caution or Watts's ambivalence. Psychedelics were no longer just being studied or philosophically interpreted. They were becoming part of a cultural identity, and almost at the same moment another kind of teacher arrived in America.

## Prabhupada arrives

[A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada](https://iskcon.org/srila-prabhupada/) arrived in New York in 1965 and formally established the [International Society for Krishna Consciousness](https://iskcon.org/history/) in 1966. He was not a psychedelic philosopher and was not proposing another chemical doorway into transcendence. He was a teacher in the Gaudiya Vaishnava tradition, carrying a devotional philosophy centred on Krishna that traced itself back through centuries of theology and practice.

The timing is hard to ignore. Just as Prabhupada was establishing ISKCON, the American counterculture was exploding, and within months these worlds physically collided. In 1967, ISKCON established a centre in San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury district, right in the middle of the counterculture.

On January 29, 1967, the Mantra-Rock Dance took place at the Avalon Ballroom. The lineup included the Grateful Dead, Moby Grape, Big Brother and the Holding Company with Janis Joplin, while [Allen Ginsberg](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poets/allen-ginsberg) also participated. And there was Prabhupada, leading kirtan in the middle of it.

The image is almost too perfect for the period: rock bands, psychedelic culture, an Indian swami, Allen Ginsberg, and thousands of young Americans chanting Hare Krishna. These were not separate cultural stories. They were literally in the same room.

The interesting part is that Prabhupada was not telling them to go deeper into psychedelics. Quite the opposite. ISKCON required devotees to give up intoxicants, so the relationship between psychedelia and Krishna consciousness is more interesting than the usual shorthand about hippies discovering Eastern religion.

Psychedelics may have made certain questions urgent. Prabhupada was offering a framework, a community and a daily practice — not another trip, but a way of life. That distinction is probably what I find most compelling. Hofmann discovered the chemical doorway. Huxley tried to understand what might be behind it. Watts tried to interpret the territory. Leary helped turn the doorway into a mass movement. Prabhupada arrived with a different proposition altogether: you do not need to keep opening the door chemically. There is a discipline you can live inside.

## And then a Beatle amplified the signal

[George Harrison](https://www.georgeharrison.com/) became closely associated with Krishna devotion and, by 1969, was working directly with Hare Krishna devotees in London. He produced the Radha Krishna Temple's recording of the Hare Krishna mantra, and later continued working with the devotees on *The Radha Krsna Temple* album.

Then came Harrison's own [*My Sweet Lord*](https://www.georgeharrison.com/releases/my-sweet-lord/), weaving the Hare Krishna mantra into one of the most recognizable songs of his career. At that point the story starts to feel almost surreal: Vivekananda in Chicago, Yogananda teaching meditation in America, Hofmann in a Swiss laboratory, Huxley writing about mescaline and perception, Watts interpreting Eastern philosophy, Leary turning psychedelics into a cultural force, Prabhupada arriving in New York, kirtan in Haight-Ashbury, and George Harrison putting Krishna consciousness into popular music. It is not a straight causal chain, but it is not random either.

## Coming around the circle from the other side

This is where the history became personal for me. I have had psychedelic experiences in which my ordinary sense of identity disappeared, and I have experienced states of interconnectedness, timelessness and something I could only describe at the time as deeply spiritual.

I have read Hofmann. I have read Huxley. I have listened to Watts. I have spent years thinking about consciousness without feeling the need to force those experiences into one religious explanation. Then, much later, Krishna consciousness entered my life from another direction.

When I started reading its history, I found Haight-Ashbury, the Mantra-Rock Dance, Ginsberg, Harrison, Huxley, Hofmann, Watts and, going even further back, Vivekananda in Chicago in 1893. Suddenly these were not isolated chapters anymore. They looked more like parts of a century-long conversation between India and the West about consciousness, identity and spiritual experience, and somehow I had wandered through several rooms of that conversation without realizing they belonged to the same house.

I do not think psychedelics prove Krishna. That conclusion would be too easy. People across cultures have interpreted mystical and psychedelic experiences through radically different frameworks, and the experience deserves to be taken seriously without pretending that one interpretation has automatically been proven by it.

But I also do not think the cultural connection should be dismissed. The psychedelic generation became deeply interested in questions that Indian traditions had been exploring through philosophy, meditation and devotional practice for centuries. At exactly that historical moment, teachers carrying those traditions were in America saying, in different ways, that those questions were worth taking seriously.

The title of Huxley's book feels more relevant to me now than it did before. The real question may never have been whether the doors of perception could be opened. Clearly they could. The harder question was always what to do after walking through them.

And that question turns out to extend far beyond psychedelics.

Because once something genuinely changes the observer, the observer cannot return completely to what existed before.

You cannot unread a book.

You cannot unhave an experience.

You cannot unknow that another way of seeing was possible.

You can reinterpret it. Doubt it. Spend years trying to understand it. But it becomes part of the person doing the interpreting.

That realization led me somewhere I did not expect when I started researching this history. I began wondering whether the same principle applies to everything else we carry: songs, films, people, losses, coincidences, stories — things encountered decades apart that suddenly touch one another and become visible as parts of the same life.

That became another essay.

[*I Believe*](/posts/i-believe) begins where this one ends: not with a claim that I know what the pattern means, but with the recognition that once something enters our story, everything that follows encounters a slightly different person.

I started reading about how ISKCON reached America and ended up tracing a path from Chicago in 1893, through laboratories in Switzerland, through Huxley's *The Doors of Perception*, through psychedelic America and Haight-Ashbury, through Watts, Prabhupada and George Harrison, and eventually back into questions I have been carrying myself for years.

What a circle to come around.

Life has humbled me.

Huxley ends *The Doors of Perception* with a line that feels like the right place to end this too:

> “He will be wiser but less cocksure... humbler in acknowledging his ignorance.”
