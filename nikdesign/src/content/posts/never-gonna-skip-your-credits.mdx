I just finished watching *Ralph Breaks the Internet*.

And as I always do, I sat through the credits.

Not because I was waiting for a post-credits scene. Not because I expected a teaser for the next movie.

I stayed because I've worked in animation.

I've been one of those names scrolling across the screen.

I know what those names represent.

And then Disney fucking Rickrolled me.

## The Rickroll nobody might see

At the end of *Ralph Breaks the Internet*, the film teases a sneak peek of *Frozen 2*.

You think you're about to see something from the next big Disney movie.

Instead, Ralph appears, performing Rick Astley's *Never Gonna Give You Up*.

A fully animated Rickroll.

Not the original music video playing on a screen. Not a recycled internet clip.

An actual animated performance featuring Ralph.

It's hilarious.

But here's what struck me.

**Somebody had to make that.**

And not just somebody. An entire production pipeline had to bring that sequence to life.

For a joke that many people would never discover.

Not because it wasn't funny.

Not because it wasn't worth watching.

But because most people had already left.

## Every frame is accounted for

I studied animation. I've worked in animation production. I know what goes into making even a seemingly simple sequence.

People sometimes imagine that once you've built a 3D character, you just tell the computer what to do.

That's not how animation works.

An animator chooses the poses. The timing. The spacing. The anticipation. The performance.

Even when software interpolates between keyframes, the creative decisions belong to the artist.

A computer can calculate what happens between two positions.

It cannot decide, on its own, what makes a performance funny, charming, awkward, or emotionally convincing in the context of the story.

And animation is only one part of the process.

There are storyboards, layouts, character rigs, lighting, rendering, compositing, editing, sound, reviews, revisions, and the production coordination that keeps everything moving.

All of those departments contribute to the finished result.

Every shot has to work.

Every frame is accounted for.

Even when that frame exists solely to make someone laugh.

And in this case, the artists did all that work knowing the sequence would appear after the credits.

Think about that.

**They put the work in without any guarantee that the audience would ever see it.**

## And then the platform tells you to skip

Here's where the story gets even more absurd.

Disney+ has a feature that encourages viewers to skip the credits and move on.

A convenient little interface telling you that the movie is effectively finished.

And depending on how that prompt behaves, following it can mean missing the very sequence Disney's artists worked so hard to create.

Let that sink in.

**Disney paid artists to animate a Rickroll that Disney+ might encourage you never to see.**

I'm not suggesting somebody at Disney deliberately set out to hide the sequence.

That's almost worse, in a way.

It doesn't require anyone to make that decision.

One part of the organization invests enormous effort into creating an experience.

Another part optimizes the experience of moving past it.

And both are doing exactly what their respective systems are designed to do.

The contradiction emerges all by itself.

The artists say, *There's still something here worth seeing.*

The interface says, *Would you like to move on?*

## Why I stay

I've worked on productions associated with Disney and Warner Bros.

I worked at Legend3D in Toronto, including as an Assistant Team Lead.

And yes, I have an [IMDb page](https://www.imdb.com/name/nm11338371/).

I'm glad I do.

Because companies don't last forever.

Offices close. Productions move. Teams are dissolved. People lose their jobs. The business carries on, often without the people who helped create its success.

Legend3D's Toronto office closed in 2019.

I remember what that meant.

But the films are still there.

And so are the credits.

Those names aren't decoration.

They represent artists, technicians, coordinators, supervisors, production assistants, and countless other people whose work made the film possible.

Some contributed to sequences that became iconic.

Others worked on moments that audiences barely noticed.

Some spent months solving problems that, when solved correctly, became completely invisible.

That's the nature of production.

When everything works, the audience sees the movie.

They don't see the work.

And that's fine. That's what we're trying to achieve.

But when the credits roll, the illusion gives way to the people who built it.

**The credits are where the movie acknowledges its makers.**

That's why I watch them.

## The people behind the joke

Now imagine being one of the artists assigned to that Rickroll.

You still have to deliver.

The performance still needs to be right.

Ralph still has to feel like Ralph.

The animation still has to pass review.

The lighting still needs to work. The render still needs to finish. The sound and picture still need to come together.

Nobody gets to say, *Don't worry about it. It's after the credits. Most people won't see it.*

At least, that's not how good artists approach their work.

You make it properly because it's your work.

Because the character deserves it.

Because the audience member who does stay deserves it.

And because there's a certain pride in knowing that even the smallest contribution carries the same care as the rest of the film.

That's what I love about this sequence.

Not merely that Disney understood the Rickroll.

Not merely that the gag was funny.

**It's that people cared enough to make it properly, even though they couldn't know how many people would ever see it.**

## The strange business of making art

There's an uncomfortable relationship between art and the systems that finance and distribute it.

A studio can spend years producing a film.

Thousands of people can devote extraordinary skill and attention to every detail.

Then the distribution platform can reduce the entire experience to a thumbnail, a progress bar, and a button suggesting what to watch next.

I'm not against convenience.

Sometimes I skip credits too, especially when I'm watching something I've seen before.

And I understand why streaming platforms offer the option.

But I think we've become a little too comfortable treating the credits as disposable.

As though the movie ends when the story ends.

As though the people who made it are an administrative detail.

They're not.

The credits are part of the film.

And sometimes, as *Ralph Breaks the Internet* demonstrates, the filmmakers have one last thing waiting for you.

## Never gonna give you up

I didn't stay for the Rickroll.

I didn't even know it was there.

I stayed because I wanted to acknowledge the people who made the movie.

And those people had one last performance waiting for me.

A ridiculous, lovingly animated joke that could easily have gone unseen.

I laughed.

Then I thought about how much work had gone into it.

And then I thought about how many people might never discover it.

There's something beautiful about that.

An artist doesn't always know who will see their work.

They don't always know whether anyone will notice the little decisions, the extra polish, the detail that took another hour, another review, another render.

But they make those decisions anyway.

Because the work matters.

Because somebody might notice.

And because that somebody deserves the best they can give.

So to the artists who worked on that sequence:

**I saw the joke.**

**I appreciate the work.**

And to everyone whose name rolled across that screen:

I stayed for you.

I'm so fucking glad I did.

**Never gonna give you up.**

**Never gonna skip your credits.**
