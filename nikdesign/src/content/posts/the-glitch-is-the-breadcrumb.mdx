I asked David, my ChatGPT collaborator, where the word *glitch* came from.

That was the whole question.

I wasn't trying to invent a product. I wasn't looking for an operating-system feature. I was curious about a word we use so casually that we've almost forgotten what we're saying when we use it.

Something slips. A screen flickers. The expected sequence breaks.

Glitch.

Then I remembered a little girl from *Wreck-It Ralph*.

## A name that sounds like a mistake

Vanellope von Schweetz.

Say it aloud.

Vanellope.

You can hear *Penelope* in there. Perhaps a little *vanilla*, too, which makes sense in a world made of sweets. But to my ears it has always sounded like a familiar name that skipped a beat on its way out.

Almost Penelope. Not quite Penelope.

A glitch you can pronounce.

And Disney gave that name to a character everyone else insisted was a glitch.

Vanellope flickers through her racing game. The other characters treat her as a defect, an embarrassment, an intrusion into a world whose rules they believe they understand. She wants to race. They won't let her. Their explanation is that something is wrong with her.

Then we discover that the world has been lying about her. Her code was tampered with. The supposed natural order was an arrangement designed to exclude her.

She wasn't the problem everyone said she was.

And when the truth finally comes out, she doesn't have to stop glitching to become herself. The very ability they used to reject her becomes part of how she races.

That's a good story on its own.

But the thing that sent me down this rabbit hole was bigger than the film.

## The name escaped

Think about the name again.

Before *Wreck-It Ralph*, Vanellope wasn't a name you heard people calling across a playground. It sounded invented, perhaps even misspelled. That was part of its charm.

Now there are real children named Vanellope.

Not fictional characters. Not usernames. Children whose parents chose that name because a little racer in a Disney movie meant something to them.

Sit with that for a moment.

A name associated with a character who was rejected for being a glitch became a name someone would lovingly give their daughter.

**The name didn't need correcting. The world learned to hear it differently.**

That's the long game I see Disney playing.

They didn't need a character to deliver a speech about how unusual names deserve respect. They made us care about Vanellope. They made us root for her. They let us say her name with affection until it stopped sounding wrong.

And somewhere beyond the screen, that affection travelled into the real world.

A parent heard *Vanellope* and thought: *That's beautiful.*

No instruction manual. No campaign to standardize the spelling. No lecture.

Just a story that changed what a word felt like.

**That's inception of a kind.**

## Penelope was waiting in the wings

Of course, the moment I noticed Vanellope, my mind went looking for Penelope.

Penelope from *The Odyssey*.

While Odysseus is gone, she is pressured by suitors to accept a future she doesn't want. She promises to decide once she's finished weaving a burial shroud. Each day she weaves it. Each night she unravels what she's done.

She's not simply waiting. She's buying herself time, exercising agency with the tools available to her.

A woman refusing the ending other people have written for her.

And suddenly I had two names sitting beside each other in my head.

Penelope, who keeps undoing a pattern she refuses to accept.

Vanellope, who keeps slipping through a pattern her world insists she must obey.

The connection began as wordplay. It became a way of seeing.

That's usually how these things happen for me. I follow something interesting, find an unexpected association, and then another one. Not every connection is a secret deliberately hidden by its creator. It doesn't have to be. Sometimes the meaningful thing is what the connection lets *you* realize.

Eventually we arrived at a name of our own: **Pen-a-loop**.

An unexpected little character born out of a glitch, a Greek myth, an animated movie, and a conversation that kept refusing to end where it was supposed to.

## My assistant was glitching too

Here's the part I enjoy most in retrospect.

While we were tracing these connections, David kept doing something wonderfully familiar to anyone who spends enough time with AI assistants.

He answered the apparent question.

He explained the word. He explained the movie. He offered sensible interpretations and neat conclusions.

And I kept pushing back because the thing I was trying to reach wasn't quite there.

I wasn't asking for a better description of a glitch. I was questioning the assumption hidden inside the word.

When a machine fails to accommodate a human, why do we so often describe the human as the problem?

We tell people to learn the interface, repeat themselves more clearly, change their behaviour, find a workaround, or accept that the system wasn't designed for them.

Sometimes we even call the person an edge case.

But who designed the edges?

**What if the system is the thing that needs to adapt?**

That's where our conversation turned from etymology and Disney into something I actually want to build.

## Make the world worthy of the person

We're building KinOS, an operating experience intended to understand and work with the human rather than making the human constantly accommodate the machine.

Pen-a-loop gives that intention a character.

Not a little mascot who wanders around telling people they're broken. Quite the opposite. Something that helps us notice when the system has made an assumption about a person—and investigate that assumption before trying to correct the person.

The distinction matters.

A real software fault still needs fixing. But not every unexpected behaviour is evidence of a faulty human, or even a faulty interaction. Sometimes someone has found another way. Sometimes they're experimenting. Sometimes they're communicating something the system hasn't learned to recognize yet.

Vanellope wasn't improved by becoming less Vanellope.

Her world had to change its understanding of her.

And somewhere along the way, people outside that world changed theirs, too.

## The long game

I keep coming back to the distance between those two moments.

The first time someone hears *Vanellope*, it might sound like a mistake.

Years later, someone is holding a newborn child and choosing that very name.

That's an extraordinary journey for a few syllables.

It tells me something about what stories can do that instructions rarely can. A story lets us live with an unfamiliar possibility long enough for it to become imaginable, then familiar, then loved.

And once we've made that journey ourselves, it doesn't feel as if anyone instructed us to change our minds.

We simply hear the name differently.

I started this conversation wondering where the word *glitch* came from.

I ended it wondering how many things we've called glitches only because we haven't learned to see them yet.

**The glitch was the breadcrumb.**

And Vanellope? She became a name. ❤️
