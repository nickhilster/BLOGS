We talk about values as if we chose them privately.

We say *my values*, as if each of us sat down one morning, weighed every possible way of living, and picked a moral system off a shelf.

I don't think that's how it actually happens. Most values arrive before we even have the words for them.

They show up in the stories told around us, the expectations placed on us, the food made at home, the songs played at a wedding and at a funeral, the gestures that communicate respect, the topics a family simply doesn't discuss. They live in language, ritual, humour, architecture, clothing, mythology, music, craft, cinema, dance, and design.

Our values are distilled through our ancestors and instilled in human art and expression.

That sentence has a distinction worth sitting with. Values aren't just passed down. They're distilled.

## We don't inherit our ancestors intact

No generation gets the complete inner world of the one before it. We get fragments.

A proverb survives long after the event that produced it is forgotten. A recipe outlives the migration that changed half its ingredients. A song keeps being sung after the grief behind it has blurred into tradition. A family keeps a rule without anyone remembering whether it started as wisdom, fear, necessity, or control.

Time filters culture the same way it filters everything else. Some things get recorded. Others get quietly dropped. Some people get celebrated in the retelling. Others get written out of it. Languages disappear, rituals merge into each other, symbols get repurposed, and practices that once kept people alive turn into things you do because it's tradition.

What reaches us isn't ancestry in its original form. It's ancestry after a few centuries of pressure — war, migration, poverty, prosperity, colonization, religion, technology, love, shame, resistance, adaptation. Every generation keeps some of it, drops some of it, and changes more of it than it realizes.

That's distillation. We get what survived.

But surviving doesn't prove something was good.

## Art is where values get stored

Art gets treated like decoration around the "serious" parts of civilization — politics, economics, law, science, infrastructure. I think that gets it backwards.

Art is one of the oldest storage systems we have.

A sculpture can hold an idea of divinity. A dance can carry a community's whole relationship to courtship, discipline, gender, war, devotion. A building tells you exactly whom its architects wanted to feel powerful and whom they expected to feel small. Clothing carries ideas about modesty, status, geography, labour, rebellion, belonging.

A song can hold grief longer than anyone remembers the names of the people it was written for.

A myth can preserve a moral argument long after the actual historical facts underneath it are gone.

A film shows you what a generation feared, wanted, admired, or refused to look at directly. A product reveals whether the people who built it valued efficiency, beauty, obedience, accessibility, profit, dignity, or human agency — whether they meant to say so or not.

Art doesn't just describe a culture. It makes the culture repeatable. Someone sees an image, hears a rhythm, walks into a space, reads a story, sits through a ritual, and something gets felt before it gets understood.

That's why art can move values in a way instruction can't.

A rule tells you what a society claims to believe. Its art usually tells you what it actually believes.

## Expression carries more than beauty

We like to think of artistic inheritance as beauty, identity, pride. But expression carries everything a person puts into it: tenderness and cruelty both, liberation and hierarchy both, memory and propaganda both, the sacred and the excluded both.

A heroic story might teach courage. It might also quietly teach that violence is how legitimacy gets established. A family tradition might build belonging while reinforcing obedience at the same time. A style of architecture might inspire awe because someone designed it specifically to make ordinary people feel small in front of an institution.

Art doesn't purify a value just by preserving it. It gives the value a shape.

That shape can make an idea easier to examine, or it can make it almost impossible to question, because by the time you try, the idea is woven into a beloved song, a religious image, a national story, a family ritual, and questioning it starts to feel like rejecting the people who carried it to you.

That's where inheritance gets complicated. Respecting our ancestors doesn't mean agreeing with every conclusion they reached. They were living inside conditions we never lived through. Some of their values were sane responses to dangers that don't exist anymore. Some customs protected people under real pressure and then just stayed, long after the pressure was gone. And some inherited values were never protective in the first place. They were just power, dressed as wisdom.

Telling the difference is on us now.

## We're not just receiving this

Culture gets described like something handed down a chain, but inheritance isn't one-directional. Every generation edits what it gets.

Sometimes on purpose: an artist retells a myth from the point of view of the character who used to be silent in it, a musician runs an inherited form through new technology, a designer takes a familiar symbol and gives it a different political meaning.

Sometimes it happens without anyone deciding anything: a ritual gets shorter because city life doesn't leave room for the old version, a language absorbs foreign words, a garment goes from everyday wear to ceremonial-only, a story's uncomfortable parts quietly fall away because later tellers keep choosing the cleaner version.

Preservation is never neutral. It always includes interpretation. Even copying something exactly is a decision made by a new person, in a new context, for a new audience.

Which means we're not just descendants. We're also the medium culture travels through on its way somewhere else. We're translators, and translation is a responsibility, not a formality.

What do we keep because it actually holds wisdom? What do we keep because it gives us belonging, whether or not it holds up? What do we keep only because questioning it would cost us something socially? What do we throw out because we've learned what it costs other people? What do we reinterpret so it can stay alive without staying exactly the same?

Nobody answers those in a seminar. We answer them through what we make.

## Human expression argues for what should survive

Every creative act is a selection from reality. A writer decides whose inner life gets the page. A photographer decides what's inside the frame. A filmmaker decides which suffering stays visible and which becomes background noise. A designer decides whose body, language, and ability count as "normal" in the product.

None of that is separate from values, even when the person making it isn't trying to make a moral statement. The work shows its hand anyway.

Expression isn't only a record of inheritance. It's an argument for what should keep being inherited. It says: remember this person, notice this wound, honour this god, question this authority, keep this sound alive, laugh at this, want this body, fear this stranger, imagine this future.

Art tells people where to look, and wherever a culture keeps looking, meaning builds up.

That's exactly why controlling expression has always mattered to power. Empires erase languages on purpose. Governments censor artists on purpose. Religious institutions regulate imagery on purpose. Commercial systems fund some stories and quietly starve others, also on purpose, even if nobody frames it that way.

Control the expression, and you control what people get to remember, admire, or imagine.

## The ancestor is also a job we haven't started yet

We usually picture ancestors as people behind us. But it's also a role waiting ahead of us.

Someday, we'll be the fragments somebody else reconstructs. Future people won't have our context. They'll meet us through photographs, recordings, interfaces, buildings, products, messages, films, memes, music, stories. They'll guess at our values from the systems we built and the behaviour those systems trained into people.

They'll know what we said we valued.

They'll also see what we actually funded, what we ignored, who we made visible, what we normalized, what we designed people into doing.

Our technology is part of our art, whether we think of it that way or not. Every interface is a cultural artifact. Every tool carries assumptions about attention, autonomy, trust, labour, intelligence, and what a person is worth. A frictionless product reveals a value. So does a deliberate piece of friction someone chose to leave in. A system built to maximize engagement is telling you something about what its builders think people are for. A system built to help someone leave with more clarity than dependency is telling you something else entirely.

The future inherits both what we made and what it did to people.

## What we carry forward

None of this is about standing outside history and declaring ourselves finished. We're not.

Our descendants will see contradictions we can't see yet. They'll question things that feel completely natural to us right now. They'll find primitive assumptions baked into our most sophisticated systems, the same way we find them in the systems we inherited.

That's not a failure. That's the same process working on us that worked on everyone before us.

Values move through people. People express them. Expression gives them a shape that can travel, and a shape that can be challenged.

I don't think the job is to preserve the past untouched, and I don't think it's to reject everything just because it's old. The job is discernment: look closely at what reached you, ask what actually produced it, and learn to tell wisdom from fear, belonging from exclusion, discipline from control, reverence from submission, continuity from mere repetition.

Then make something out of what you've learned. That's the only way culture keeps going, not as a sealed object passed hand to hand, but as a living interpretation that has to pass through somebody, every single time.

Our ancestors distilled their values out of the conditions of their lives and put them into stories, symbols, rituals, songs, structures, ways of making meaning. We inherited the forms before we understood what was inside them.

Now they're moving through us.

What we keep, what we change, and what we refuse becomes part of what we hand off next.

One day, nobody will be around to explain any of this in person.

They'll still have what we made.
