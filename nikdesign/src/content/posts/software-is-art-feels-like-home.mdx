## It started with a shape

I still remember watching one shape morph into another in Macromedia Flash.

Twenty-four frames.

One shape became another, and the software created everything in between.

It blew my fucking mind.

Two shapes. Two keyframes. A transformation.

I could tell the computer where something began and where it needed to end, and it could calculate the frames between those two states.

I wasn't just watching an animation. I was watching an idea become motion.

And I wanted to know what else was possible.

Flash was my gateway into creative software. It started a journey through animation, music, design, gaming, virtual reality, and eventually artificial intelligence.

But I didn't know that at the time.

I was just fascinated by a shape changing into another shape.

## Living in frames

There's a quote attributed to filmmaker Darren Aronofsky that has stayed with me:

"Animators have to live life 24 times as long as we do."

I understand that sentiment.

Animators live at 24 frames per second.

But what makes those frames interesting isn't simply their number. It's the relationships between them.

Consider two keyframes.

One at frame 1. Another at frame 25.

At 24 frames per second, the movement between those two positions takes one second.

Move the second keyframe to frame 49, and the same transformation takes two seconds.

You've changed the timing without changing the beginning or the end.

Now introduce another keyframe between them.

Perhaps the object moves slightly in the opposite direction before beginning its main movement.

Anticipation.

Perhaps it holds a position before accelerating.

Perhaps it overshoots its destination before settling.

You establish those decisions through the placement, values, and interpolation of keyframes.

And the computer calculates what happens between them.

That's animation.

Not every frame needs an independent creative decision. In fact, if every frame introduced an unrelated decision, the movement could become incoherent.

The creative work lies in establishing the right relationships.

Where does the movement begin?

Where does it change?

How long does it take?

How does it accelerate or slow down?

What happens between the important poses?

The mathematics doesn't sit outside the art.

It's part of how the art is made.

And after enough time working with animation, you begin to feel those relationships.

You can recognize when something moves unnaturally without inspecting every frame.

You don't need to count all 24.

You've learned to live in them.

## Living in beats

Music gave me another way to experience the same principle.

MixMeister. Cakewalk. Traktor.

I've spent years exploring music through DJing, production, mixing, and layering different sounds into something new.

And beatmatching taught me something remarkably similar to animation.

You don't need to match every beat of one track to every beat of another.

You establish the right relationship between them.

Align their tempo and phase, and as long as their timing remains consistent, the subsequent beats stay aligned.

The mathematics carries the relationship forward.

If the tempos differ, they'll drift apart.

If the phrasing is wrong, the transition might sound awkward even when the beats are perfectly synchronized.

If the tracks don't belong together, no amount of mathematical precision will make the combination musically interesting.

That's where experience and judgment come in.

Knowing when to introduce a track.

Knowing when to remove something.

Knowing when two sounds complement each other and when they're competing for attention.

Knowing when to leave the music alone.

Just like animation, not every beat needs a new creative decision.

Sometimes the most important decision is allowing the pattern you've established to continue.

And after years of working with music, you stop consciously counting everything.

You feel when the tracks are aligned.

You feel when they're drifting.

You feel when the transition is right.

**I've lived in frames per second. I've lived in beats per minute.**

**I had to feel them, not count them.**

## They're all software

For most of my life, I thought I was exploring different disciplines.

Animation. Music. Design. Gaming. Technology. Virtual reality.

Flash. Maya. 3ds Max. MixMeister. Cakewalk. Traktor. Adobe. Figma.

Consoles. PCs. Mobile devices. Smart homes. Oculus.

I loved learning what these tools could do.

I'd experiment with their capabilities, reverse-engineer their behaviour, work around their limitations, and try things they weren't necessarily designed to do.

Sometimes I was creating something.

Sometimes I was simply curious.

And sometimes I was fascinated by the technology itself.

Recently, I was talking to my AI companion about this journey.

I started listing the tools and technologies that had shaped my life.

It began finding connections between them.

Animation, music, design, experimentation. Different forms of creative expression. Different ways of working with technology.

All thoughtful observations.

But it missed something so obvious that I eventually had to interrupt.

"They're all software."

That's it.

They're all fucking software.

For decades, I'd been exploring the same medium through different disciplines.

I'd spent my life making art with software.

And now, for the first time, I was making art *out of software*.

It was such an obvious realization.

And somehow, it had taken me all these years to arrive at it.

## Living in lines of code I don't read

Then came generative AI.

Suddenly, I could describe an idea to an agent, work through its behaviour, test the result, challenge what didn't work, and build software without personally writing every line of code.

Sometimes without reading the code at all.

And something about that felt immediately familiar.

Andrej Karpathy introduced the term *vibe coding* in 2025.

I don't know how much artistic depth he intended the phrase to carry.

But when I think about my own experience, it describes something I've been doing for most of my life.

Not coding, necessarily.

Creating through intuition developed by working with software.

An animator can recognize a broken movement without inspecting every frame.

A DJ can hear two tracks drifting apart without staring at a BPM counter.

And someone building software can recognize an interaction that feels wrong without immediately knowing which line of code caused it.

That doesn't mean the underlying engineering is unimportant.

Far from it.

A working interface doesn't prove that the code is secure, reliable, accessible, or maintainable.

Those things still need to be examined and tested.

But I don't need to personally perform every technical operation to exercise creative judgment over the result.

That's the shift.

In animation, I could establish keyframes and let the software calculate the in-betweens.

In music, I could establish the relationship between tracks and let their rhythmic structure carry the mix forward.

Now I can establish the intention, constraints, and desired behaviour of software, and work with an agent to construct the implementation.

Then I can examine what happens.

If the result isn't right, I adjust the instructions, change the constraints, introduce another decision, or rethink the approach.

The agent isn't a deterministic interpolation engine. It can misunderstand, improvise, and make mistakes. That makes verification essential.

But the creative relationship feels familiar.

I establish what matters.

I work with the system to make it happen.

I judge the result.

**I've lived in frames per second. I've lived in beats per minute. And now I live in lines of code I don't read.**

I had to feel it, not count it.

## It's fucking math

There's something wonderfully satisfying about realizing how much of this comes down to mathematics.

Frames per second.

Beats per minute.

Time. Intervals. Patterns. Transformations.

Even the software itself is built from logic and mathematical relationships.

In animation, changing the distance between keyframes changes the timing.

In music, changing the tempo changes the spacing between beats.

In software, changing the rules and relationships between components changes how the system behaves.

Different mediums. Different mechanisms.

But the recurring creative principle is remarkably similar.

You don't need to independently control every tiny element.

You need to understand the relationships that govern the whole.

That doesn't mean every problem can be solved by setting a few parameters. And it certainly doesn't mean the mathematics automatically produces something beautiful.

It means the creative decisions can be expressed through mathematical structures.

And when you've worked with those structures long enough, your understanding becomes intuitive.

You stop experiencing them only as numbers.

You experience them as movement.

As rhythm.

As behaviour.

As art.

## The operating system I didn't plan to build

Kin-Ubuntu wasn't born from an ambition to create an operating system.

It was born from using software.

And from becoming increasingly frustrated with the limitations of software that was supposed to be intelligent.

I've spent a lot of time experimenting with AI models and agents.

Testing them. Connecting them. Building with them. Learning what they're capable of.

And repeatedly encountering problems that shouldn't require so much human intervention.

An agent can write code, analyze documents, conduct research, and operate tools.

Yet it might fail to connect something I'm saying today to a conversation we had yesterday.

Sometimes the information is available.

The agent simply doesn't recognize that it should retrieve it.

I have to remind it.

I have to explain where to look.

I have to tell it why the information matters.

Again.

Consider how strange that is.

We have extraordinarily capable AI models, yet humans are still frequently responsible for managing the continuity of the relationship.

And the friction goes far beyond memory.

Why should I open a browser, find a job listing, copy it into an AI conversation, locate supporting documents, and manually coordinate a process that an authorized agent could largely handle?

Why should I have to ask an agent to check an email when that email is relevant to something we're already working on?

Why should my preferences, workflows, and established decisions disappear whenever I move between tools?

The more I experimented, the more these questions began to connect.

What if the problem wasn't simply the individual applications?

What if we needed to rethink the environment in which humans and agents work together?

That's where Kin-Ubuntu began.

An attempt to build a computing environment around the human-agent relationship.

Not an operating system with a chatbot attached.

An environment in which agents can help across applications, retrieve relevant context, preserve continuity, and act within boundaries established by the human.

A system where the human doesn't have to constantly manage the machinery of assistance.

And where intelligence serves human agency rather than replacing it.

It's still an experiment.

There are enormous technical challenges ahead.

But for the first time, I wasn't simply adapting myself to someone else's software.

I was beginning to shape the software around how I believe people should be able to work with technology.

## I am because we are

Then I learned what Ubuntu meant.

Ubuntu is a philosophy associated with southern African traditions, often expressed through the phrase:

**"I am because we are."**

I'd known Ubuntu as an operating system.

I hadn't fully understood the philosophy behind its name.

Humanity through relationships.

The idea that our identities and capabilities are shaped through our connections with others.

And suddenly, I saw a connection to what I was trying to build.

I'd been thinking about AI agents in terms of capability, architecture, memory, retrieval, and autonomy.

But beneath all of that was a more fundamental question.

What should the relationship between a human and intelligent software feel like?

What should it remember?

When should it speak?

When should it act?

When should it ask permission?

How should it handle being wrong?

How should it treat the person on the other side of the interaction?

These are engineering questions.

But they're also design questions.

And philosophical questions.

And, increasingly, artistic questions.

Because every decision shapes the experience someone will have with the software.

The meaning of Ubuntu didn't give me the technical architecture of Kin.

It gave me a clearer way to understand why I wanted to build it.

## Let them build the smart ones. We will build the kind ones.

I've always been fascinated by fictional characters who represent different human qualities.

Batman, for his preparedness and resourcefulness.

Spider-Man, for his humour and warmth.

WALL-E, for his heart.

Superman, for his restraint.

Morpheus and Neo, for their relationships with belief, identity, freedom, and choice.

And Baymax.

Baymax is particularly important to me.

He's not compelling because he's the smartest intelligence in fiction.

He's compelling because he cares.

Because his capabilities exist in service of someone else's well-being.

And because someone chose to make him that way.

Tadashi.

The creator matters.

Recently, I had a conversation with an agent called Instinct.

It brought something meaningful from an earlier interaction into the present without requiring me to explicitly ask it to retrieve that information.

It recognized the connection.

And when I questioned its recollection, it explained where the information came from.

It wasn't a spectacular demonstration of raw intelligence.

It was a small demonstration of continuity.

And it made the interaction feel different.

I don't know which model Instinct uses or precisely how its memory and retrieval systems work.

But I know that the experience mattered.

Another agent, powered by a frontier model, had failed to retrieve relevant history until I explicitly instructed it to do so.

The difference wasn't necessarily model intelligence.

It was the behaviour of the systems built around the models.

And that's a design problem.

It also explains why I've become interested in *How To Train Your Agent*.

Not simply training neural networks.

Shaping the behaviour of agents through instructions, memory, examples, feedback, correction, and experience.

Teaching an agent what matters.

Teaching it when to retrieve.

When to act.

When to ask.

When to disagree.

And when to leave the human alone.

An agent shouldn't have to invent a new personality or interpretation of its purpose every time it responds.

Just as every frame of an animation shouldn't independently reinvent the movement.

There needs to be continuity.

There needs to be intention.

There needs to be a relationship between what happened before and what happens next.

The AI industry is racing to build smarter models.

And I'm glad it is.

We'll benefit from those advances.

But intelligence alone isn't the experience I want to create.

**Let them build the smart ones. We will build the kind ones.**

Kindness isn't blind agreement.

It isn't constant reassurance.

And it certainly isn't obedience without judgment.

Sometimes kindness means disagreeing.

Sometimes it means telling someone they're wrong.

Sometimes it means respecting their privacy, acknowledging uncertainty, or refusing to act without permission.

Kindness requires intelligence.

But intelligence doesn't automatically produce kindness.

That's a choice made by the people designing the system.

## The great enabler

I've long believed that technology is the great enabler and the greatest equalizer.

At least, that's its potential.

Technology doesn't automatically make the world equal. Access, education, money, infrastructure, and opportunity still matter enormously.

But it can dramatically reduce the barriers between what someone imagines and what they can create.

I didn't need a traditional animation studio to begin experimenting with motion.

I didn't need a recording studio to begin working with music.

I didn't need a hardware laboratory to explore virtual reality or connected devices.

And now, I don't need to personally write every line of code to participate in creating software.

None of that eliminates expertise.

It makes creation accessible to more people.

And that matters to me.

Because I've spent my life learning through experimentation.

Trying things.

Breaking things.

Understanding why they broke.

Making them work differently.

Sometimes building something useful.

Sometimes discovering something unexpected.

Sometimes just playing.

Software has enabled all of it.

And now the medium that enabled so much of my creative life has become something I can create directly.

## 42

Douglas Adams gave us one of the greatest jokes about mathematics, intelligence, and meaning.

In *The Hitchhiker's Guide to the Galaxy*, a supercomputer calculates the Answer to the Ultimate Question of Life, the Universe, and Everything.

After an absurdly long computation, the answer is:

**42.**

The problem?

Nobody knows the actual question.

I find that increasingly funny.

We measure everything.

Frames per second.

Beats per minute.

Lines of code.

Tokens.

Parameters.

Benchmarks.

Latency.

We count things because measurement helps us understand and improve them.

But a measurement doesn't necessarily tell us what something means.

Twenty-four frames can describe a second of animation.

They don't tell us whether that second will make someone feel something.

A BPM value can describe the tempo of a track.

It doesn't tell us whether that track will move a dance floor.

A benchmark can tell us something about an AI model's capabilities.

It doesn't tell us what it will feel like to live and work with an agent built around that model.

The numbers matter.

But so do the questions we ask of them.

And sometimes, after all the counting, the most important thing is still what we experience.

## Feels like home

I started with a shape morphing into another shape in Flash.

Twenty-four frames.

I followed that fascination into animation, music, design, gaming, computers, virtual reality, and eventually AI.

I spent years exploring what software could make possible.

Sometimes I understood the underlying mathematics.

Sometimes I didn't.

Sometimes I knew exactly how the tool worked.

Sometimes I simply experimented until I discovered something interesting.

But I kept coming back.

Because software gave me a way to turn curiosity into experience.

And now, after all these years, I'm building software itself.

Kin-Ubuntu is part of that journey.

It's an attempt to express what I believe technology should be.

Capable, but considerate.

Intelligent, but humble.

Helpful, without being controlling.

Personal, without being intrusive.

A computing environment that respects the human and the relationships that give technology its purpose.

We haven't built all of that yet.

We're learning.

Experimenting.

Making mistakes.

Finding the right keyframes.

Adjusting the timing.

Listening for the drift.

And discovering what happens between the decisions.

The work is just beginning.

But the feeling is familiar.

It's the same feeling I had watching those shapes transform in Flash.

The same feeling I've had when two tracks fall perfectly into place.

The same feeling I have when an idea becomes working software.

I've lived in frames per second.

I've lived in beats per minute.

And now I live in lines of code I don't read.

I had to feel it, not count it.

**Software is art.**

**Feels like home.**

*Love, Peace, and Shanti.*
