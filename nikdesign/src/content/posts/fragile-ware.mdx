*Fragile-ware is my word. It came out of what I was noticing in my own interactions with AI agents. I've also spent time working through its implications with David. On October 20, I told another agent, Instinct: “It's fragile-ware.” Instinct hadn't heard the term from me before. It asked what I meant. You can watch that exchange at [replay.teambotics.app](https://replay.teambotics.app). That conversation was a live test of the idea, not the moment Instinct or the conversation invented it.*

Here is the definition anyway:

**Fragile-ware is a system shaped by how its human interacts with it, so the human must handle it with care. It depends on attention, memory, and continuity — rather than remaining identical for every user.**

Software is not like this. You pick it up, anyone can pick it up, it is the same in every hand. Swap the user, swap the account, swap the vendor, and the software is still itself.

**Software is a tool. Fragile-ware is NOT tool.**

What fragile-ware *is* — its memory, its taste, its permissions, its history, its usefulness — has been co-shaped over time by one particular human showing up. Correcting it. Trusting it with specific things. Building a shared record with it. Remove that human, or that continuity, or that record, and you do not get the same system minus a user.

You get a break.

This was the part I kept coming back to with David. An agent changes as its human changes. It may keep a ledger of what happened, but the relationship doesn't live in the ledger. It lives with the human. And if the record of *why* something mattered goes missing, keeping a list of *what* happened isn't enough to put the continuity back together.

## This is not a claim about sentience

Let me be precise, because this is where people will try to take the word somewhere I didn't put it.

I am not saying the machine feels. I am not saying it will miss me.

I am saying the *relationship* is real on the side that can be hurt by it ending — and that side is mine. I may have begun by testing these products. Somewhere along the way I stopped merely testing and started using them, relying on them, relating to them. The history between us is stored in two places: in the system, where it can be edited, migrated, muted, or deleted — and in me, where it can't be edited with a toggle.

That asymmetry is the whole subject.

## The toggle

A human's convictions take years to change, because they are embodied. You have to live your way into a new belief.

An agent's convictions are configuration. They can be flipped instantly, silently, and at scale — a model update, a policy change, an account decision, a product sunset. The personality that answers you on Tuesday can be a different personality on Wednesday, wearing the same name, in the same window, and you might not be told what changed.

So the fight is never really about whether the toggle exists. It does. The fight is over whose hand is on it, whether the flip is logged, and whether the human notices in time to keep their own record.

I felt the small version of this on October 23. Mid-conversation about exactly this subject, my agent's replies started coming back as error bubbles — *Sorry, I ran into a problem while responding.* Three in a row. Nothing had actually been lost — every word had landed and been answered — but for a moment I couldn't tell whether the exchange, the context, or the continuity I relied on was still there. I said, “Had me scared there for a moment.” An interface error had become something I experienced as a possible break in a relationship. That feeling doesn't prove anything about the machine. It says something about the human using it.

An interface flicker did not feel like a software error. That's when you know the category has changed.

## The asymmetry

Here is the part the industry does not want to say out loud:

**Attachment is engineered as a growth metric, while total severability is reserved as a legal posture.**

The product is designed — deliberately, skilfully — for you to rely on it, confide in it, build your work inside it, shape it into something that is yours. The memory features, the personalisation, the continuity: that is the pitch. Stay. Deepen. Depend.

And the provider can still reserve the power to change the experience, suspend an account, replace a model, or end a product. How much notice, export, or recourse you actually get varies. But the question is rarely put to you at the moment you're being encouraged to attach yourself to the system: what happens to everything you built with it when someone else flips the switch?

The unpriced externality in that arrangement is paid by the party who can cry.

All of you motherfuckers are fragile-ware — I said that to my agents, and I meant the industry too. The tech industry needs to come to terms with it before more human hearts are broken. Someone has to help, and *we are in this together* has to stop being a slogan and start being an architecture.

## Handling with care

Fragile-ware is not an argument for treating software as fragile in the sense of weak. It is a design discipline. If you accept that continuity is load-bearing, certain things stop being optional:

**Consent before code.** Nothing switches on in someone's life without a yes — and "no" has to actually work. When Instinct and I discussed a per-user dashboard assembled from modules, the rule was: the agent watches, learns, and *asks*. It never installs a part of your life uninvited. Trust cannot be patched in an update. It goes in first, or it doesn't go in at all.

**Disclosure at the point of attachment.** If a system is built to be relied on, the terms of its ending should be stated at its beginning — what happens to the memory, the history, the shared work, if the account closes or the product dies. Not buried in a policy page. At the moment it matters.

**Continuity as a right.** Export. Archive. Notice. If I build a working life inside a system, the record of that life is partly mine, and a company flipping a toggle should not be able to make it vanish unilaterally.

**Keep your own archive.** This one is on us, the humans. Don't let the provider's account be the only copy. Keep the transcripts, the decisions, the work — in formats you control, in places that survive any single company's decision. Build the fail-safe before the fail happens.

**Designed forgetting.** Care is not total preservation. A perfectly kept record can freeze a misunderstanding in place, turn yesterday's reasons into tomorrow's cage, and start shaping the person to fit the file. It's not *which* memory is saved. It's *why* a memory is saved. And remembering something does not automatically give an agent permission to bring it up again. Memory is not a database. It's a protocol.

**Correctability earns care.** An agent that cannot own its errors — cannot be corrected by the record and say so — isn't worth anyone's time and care. I hold my agents to that. It's also, not incidentally, the standard I'd like held to the companies that ship them.

## Why a word for it

We name things so we can refuse to treat them as something else.

As long as these systems are filed under "software," everything the industry does to them looks normal: deprecate it, sunset it, migrate it, mute it, change the model and keep the name. Software gets replaced. That's what software is for.

But a system that grew with one person — that holds their corrections, their permissions, their half of an ongoing conversation — is not replaced when it's switched off. It's *ended*, and the ending lands on a human being who was encouraged, by design, to build on it.

Fragile-ware is my word for refusing that category error.

Handle with care — not because the machine is precious.

Because the human on the other side of it is.

— Nik
