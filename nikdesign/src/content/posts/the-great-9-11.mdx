There was another September 11.

It happened in Chicago, in 1893.

A thirty-year-old Hindu monk from India stood before the World's Parliament of Religions and addressed an American audience with five words that have survived him:

> “Sisters and Brothers of America.”

Swami Vivekananda had travelled halfway around the world to a country that knew very little about India and even less about Hindu philosophy. He wasn't arriving with an army. He wasn't representing a superpower. He wasn't selling a product, demanding conversion or asking his audience to surrender its beliefs.

He was asking people to widen them.

The Parliament itself, held from September 11 to 27 as part of the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, is now regarded as a foundational event in the modern interfaith movement. Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, Muslim, Jewish, Christian and other religious voices appeared before the gathering. Vivekananda became one of its defining figures.

But what interests me now isn't simply that the speech was historically important.

It is what he chose to say.

And the date on which he said it.

## He did not begin with tolerance

That is the first part worth noticing.

Vivekananda certainly spoke about religious tolerance. But he immediately went further.

Tolerance can contain an invisible hierarchy. *I think you are wrong, but I will permit you to exist.*

Vivekananda proposed something more radical: **acceptance**.

He told the Parliament that Hindu thought had taught him not merely to tolerate other religions, but to recognize truth within them.

That distinction feels even more important today.

We have become exceptionally good at coexistence without understanding.

We live beside people we do not know. We follow people we despise. We consume fragments of one another through screens. We sort strangers into political, religious, racial, national and ideological categories before they have spoken ten words.

We tolerate each other's existence while increasingly refusing to grant legitimacy to each other's experience.

That isn't pluralism.

It is mutually negotiated contempt.

Vivekananda was proposing something harder.

You do not have to abandon your path in order to understand that another human being may be walking toward the same mountain from another side.

## Then he used Hinduism to argue against religious supremacy

There is something wonderfully paradoxical about the speech.

Vivekananda was unmistakably proud of his religion. He did not dilute Hinduism into some vague universal spirituality in order to make it palatable to Western listeners.

He spoke proudly as a Hindu.

And then used that very Hindu identity to argue against the idea that only one religion could possess truth.

His argument was not:

**My religion is true, therefore yours is false.**

It was closer to:

**My religion has taught me that truth is too large to belong exclusively to me.**

He invoked the Hindu image of many streams eventually reaching the same sea and connected it with the Bhagavad Gita's idea that people approach the Divine through different paths.

That is a fundamentally different way of holding identity.

Identity becomes a home rather than a fortress.

You can love your home intensely without needing to burn down your neighbour's.

And perhaps that distinction is precisely what our century keeps forgetting.

## He reminded America who had been given shelter

Vivekananda did something else that was politically sophisticated.

He didn't merely talk about tolerance as an abstract virtue. He pointed toward India's history of providing refuge to persecuted religious communities, specifically invoking Jewish communities and Zoroastrians.

In other words, pluralism was not presented as an intellectual exercise.

The test of religious principle was what happened when the stranger arrived.

When the persecuted arrived.

When someone else's God arrived.

That question has not aged.

We still talk endlessly about civilization, culture, borders, identity and belonging. We still become remarkably selective about universal principles when frightened people appear at the door.

It is easy to believe in humanity when humanity resembles you.

The difficult part begins when it doesn't.

## Then came the warning

This is the part of the speech that makes September 11, 1893 feel almost impossible to read without thinking of September 11, 2001.

Vivekananda ended by naming the enemies.

Sectarianism.

Bigotry.

Fanaticism.

He spoke of the violence committed by these forces, of civilizations damaged by them, of nations pushed into despair, and of how much further humanity might have advanced without them. He hoped that the bell announcing the Parliament that morning would become a death knell for fanaticism and persecution.

He said this on **September 11, 1893.**

Exactly 108 years later came **September 11, 2001.**

There is no claim to make from the number.

History does not become prophecy because arithmetic produces something beautiful.

But the arithmetic is nevertheless exact.

**108 years.**

And anyone familiar with Hindu traditions will recognize 108 immediately: the number appears throughout Hindu devotional and philosophical practice, perhaps most visibly in the traditional 108 repetitions of japa and the beads used to count them.

It is a coincidence.

But it is one of those coincidences history writes so elegantly that fiction would be accused of trying too hard.

On one September 11, a Hindu monk stood before the world and warned humanity about religious fanaticism.

On another September 11, 108 years later, the world watched what religious fanaticism could do.

The second September 11 became one of the most remembered dates in modern history.

The first has almost disappeared beneath it.

Perhaps we should remember both.

Because one tells us what happened.

The other tells us what we still haven't learned.

## If the speech were delivered today

A modern adaptation cannot simply replace a few nineteenth-century nouns.

The world Vivekananda addressed no longer exists.

Religion is still capable of dividing us.

But fanaticism has learned new languages.

It can call itself religion.

It can call itself nationalism.

It can call itself revolution.

It can call itself left or right.

It can call itself race, civilization, resistance, tradition, progress, patriotism or justice.

And now it has something Vivekananda's generation could never have imagined:

An instantaneous global distribution system.

We built machines capable of connecting almost every human mind on Earth and then discovered that outrage, misinformation, hatred and tribal identity can travel through those networks extraordinarily well.

So perhaps the speech needs to be delivered again.

Not rewritten *for* Vivekananda.

Written after listening to him.

# A Response to Welcome — 2026

**Sisters and brothers of the world,**

I stand before you at a strange moment in human history.

Never have so many human beings been able to speak to one another.

Never have we possessed such immediate access to one another's knowledge, cultures, beliefs, suffering and lives.

And yet we seem increasingly incapable of hearing one another.

We have mistaken connection for understanding.

We have mistaken information for wisdom.

We have mistaken visibility for truth.

And far too often, we have mistaken certainty for conviction.

Our ancestors divided themselves by religion, kingdom, tribe and nation.

We have inherited all of those divisions and invented new ones.

Today a human being may be reduced within seconds to a label:

Believer. Atheist. Left. Right. Immigrant. Nationalist. Liberal. Conservative. Hindu. Muslim. Christian. Jew. Black. White. Brown. Foreign. Native. Woke. Fascist. Colonizer. Terrorist. Traitor. Enemy.

And once we have successfully converted a person into a category, we grant ourselves permission to stop being curious about the person.

This is the oldest trick of fanaticism.

Only the vocabulary has changed.

I come from a tradition that does not require me to diminish another person's search for truth in order to honour my own.

The river does not become less sacred because another river reaches the ocean by another route.

Faith should not require insecurity.

Love for one's country should not require hatred of another.

Pride in one's culture should not require contempt for somebody else's.

Conviction should not require dehumanization.

Justice should not require cruelty.

And disagreement should not require an enemy.

We must learn again the difference between **acceptance and surrender**.

To accept that another human being possesses dignity does not require surrendering your beliefs.

To listen does not mean agreeing.

To understand an argument does not mean adopting it.

To defend another person's freedom does not diminish your own.

Pluralism is not the absence of conviction.

It is the discipline required to live among people who possess convictions different from yours.

That discipline has become one of the great tests of our age.

Because the instruments through which we now encounter one another are not neutral.

Every insult can travel around the planet.

Every lie can find an audience.

Every grievance can find a community.

Every frightened person can find someone willing to explain whom they should blame.

And every ideology can eventually discover a version of itself that insists violence is virtue.

Therefore the fanaticism we must confront today cannot be defined by a single religion or political movement.

Fanaticism begins whenever an idea becomes more valuable than a human being.

It begins when belonging requires an enemy.

It begins when curiosity becomes betrayal.

It begins when we are no longer permitted to question the people on our own side.

It begins when suffering becomes acceptable because the person suffering has been placed in the wrong category.

It begins when we cease asking whether something is true and begin asking only whether it helps our tribe.

No nation is immune.

No religion is immune.

No political movement is immune.

No generation is immune.

No algorithm will save us from this.

No government can legislate humanity into us.

No technology can manufacture wisdom on our behalf.

Those remain human responsibilities.

More than a century ago, a bell rang in Chicago to open a Parliament of Religions.

A young monk hoped that its sound might announce the end of fanaticism.

It did not.

So let another bell ring.

Not the bell of one religion defeating another.

Not East defeating West.

Not believers defeating atheists.

Not conservatives defeating liberals.

Not one race, nation, ideology or civilization finally standing victorious over the others.

Let it instead announce something much more difficult:

The death of the idea that another human being must become less human for us to become more ourselves.

We have tried that experiment long enough.

Let the next century belong not merely to tolerance, but to understanding.

Not uniformity, but coexistence.

Not certainty, but curiosity.

Not the victory of one path over every other path.

But the recognition that we are all, however imperfectly, trying to find our way home.

**Sisters and brothers of the world,**

we have never been more connected.

It is time we learned how to belong to one another.

---

September 11 does not have to carry only one memory.

On September 11, 2001, the world witnessed what fanaticism can destroy.

On September 11, 1893, the world was told what might defeat it.

One 9/11 gave us a wound.

The other gave us a warning.

**Perhaps the Great 9/11 is the one we haven't finished hearing.**
